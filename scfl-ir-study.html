This is a working paper, not a proposal. It measures how often the two-slot injured reserve cap actually binds, and how much of that a third slot would relieve, using the league’s own weekly roster data. It does not argue that anybody lost a game because of the cap — that claim is not testable with this data, and nothing here should be read as making it.

The desk has a position in the debate. The definitions, the counterfactual and the source files are set out in full so the position can be put aside and the work checked.

Where the numbers come from

ir-watch.json — one row per week, written by scripts/ir_watch.py from the Sleeper API at the time of that week, and never rewritten afterwards. Every figure below was recorded live, so none of it benefits from hindsight about who got hurt later.

Injured means Sleeper carries one of IR, Out, Doubtful, PUP, Sus, NA, COV, DNR. Questionable is not counted — including week-to-week knocks would inflate every number here. Stranded means an injured man occupying an active roster spot: he is the unit of harm in this study, a roster slot doing nothing.

Blocked means a team that cannot shelve everyone it would want to. There are two defensible readings, and they disagree on exactly three team-week of 96: whether a side with a free slot and more injured men than slots counts as blocked. This page counts it, on the grounds that a team a slot short is a slot short. A reader who prefers the stricter reading should subtract it.

Does the cap bind?

By week 6, 12 of 16 rosters are full and 18 injured players occupy active roster spots league-wide. Over six weeks, 8 of 16 teams have been a slot short at least once, across 24 of 96 team-weeks (25%).

Stranded in week 6 include Will Shipley, Terrance Ferguson, Kaytron Allen, James Conner, Caleb Douglas, Marcus Mariota, Jadarian Price, Ja'Kobi Lane.

What a third slot would have done

The counterfactual holds the injuries exactly as they fell and re-runs each week under a different cap, assuming every manager fills IR up to the cap with whoever is stranded and changes nothing else. That assumption is the model’s softest joint and is flagged again below.

Going from two slots to three removes 67% of the blocked team-weeks and 60% of the stranded player-weeks. Going to four removes 79% — the second extra slot buys roughly a fraction of what the first one does. If a line is to be drawn, three is where the curve bends.

The magnitude is small. 24 roster spots freed across six weeks — 4.0 a week, against 304 active roster spots in a 16-team, 19-man league. About 1%.

Who it helps

Median seed of the teams helped: 9.5, against a league median of 8.5. On this sample the change helps the bottom of the table more than the top.

The argument against: does it thin the wire?

The case against expansion is that the waiver wire is already bare and another slot makes it barer. The mechanism is not in dispute and is worth stating plainly, because it is arithmetic rather than opinion: a side that shelves an injured man frees an active spot, fills it from the wire, and the pool is one player smaller. Every extra slot used takes a player off waivers.

What is in dispute is the size of it. Across six weeks, the number of players on injured reserve league-wide went from 12 to 28 — 16 more men shelved — while the free-agent pool went from 503 to 496, a fall of 7. Not 16. The pass-through is partial because the rule reverses itself: a returning player has to be activated and somebody dropped, which puts a man back on the wire. Over a season the pool is close to conserved. What moves is the timing.

But a headcount is the wrong instrument, and it is the instrument both sides have been using. It counts bodies, and the complaint is about usable players. So this measures depth instead: a free agent is startable in a given week if he outscored the worst man the league actually started at his position — that is, if adding him would have improved somebody’s lineup.

A typical week offers 12 to 21 usable skill players on the entire wire. In week 5 — the last week with both figures — there were 14, and a third slot would have been taken up by 4 teams. Those teams do not take random players; they take the best available. So expansion would have absorbed about 29% of the usable wire that week, off the top of it.

That share is rising. By week 6 the number of teams who would use a third slot had reached 8. Week 6 has not been played, so there is no depth figure to set it against yet — but against week 5’s 14 usable players it would be most of the wire. That is the strongest form of the argument against expansion, and nobody in the league had put a number on it.

Both sides are partly right. The wire does thin, and the headcount hides it — 496 free agents in week 6, of whom, on the last played week, 14 were worth starting. But the withdrawal is a loan rather than a loss, and the men who would do the borrowing are mostly at the bottom of the table.

Two findings cut against the case for expansion and one cuts for it. Against: the mechanism is real and, at an injury peak, large relative to what the wire actually holds. For: it reverses, and the beneficiaries are the weak teams, not the strong ones — which is the opposite of the fairness objection that has been made against it.

Limitations

Read this before quoting any figure above.

It measures roster friction, not competitive harm. Nothing here shows a blocked team lost a game, or even lost points. Establishing that would need to know who each manager would have added with the freed slot and what that man then scored, which is unobservable. Every number above is a ceiling on inconvenience.

A blocked team is never actually stuck. There were 503 free agents in week 1. Any side can cut an injured man and move on. Blocked means made to choose, not prevented — and whether being made to choose is a flaw or the point of a roster limit is a judgement no amount of this data settles.

The benefit is concentrated. n.W.o account for 6 of the 24 freed spots (25%) and were a slot short in six of six weeks. A fair reader may ask whether a permanent rule change is warranted by one roster’s injury luck. Take that team out and the case weakens materially.

The relief looks weaker later in the season, but the trend is noisy. The share of each week’s blockage a third slot would clear has run 100%, 100%, 80%, 80%, 25%, 62% — averaging 93% over the first three weeks and 56% since. It is not a steady decline: the worst week was 5, at 25%, and week 6 recovered to 62% (5 of 8 blocked teams cleared). The mechanism is real — a side with more than one man stranded only has its line moved by a single extra slot — but on six weeks it cannot be called a trend, and it is not monotonic. Stranded counts have run 3, 5, 10, 13, 15, 18, so the problem is certainly growing even where the relief is holding up.

One season, six weeks, 96 team-weeks. Injury rates vary year to year and no claim is made that this one is typical. The greedy-filling assumption in the model is untested — a manager might hold a slot open against a worse injury later, in which case uptake above is overstated. And transactions are not observed, so we cannot see whether any blocked side actually cut a player it wanted to keep. That would be the strongest evidence of real cost, and we do not have it.

What would settle it

Transaction logs. Drops of injured players by teams at the cap against drops by teams with a slot free. If the cap forces real losses, it shows there. More weeks. The decay above is the open question, and re-running this at week 10 and week 14 answers it. Earlier seasons. The collector only began logging this year, so 2026 is all there is.

Four questions worth answering before anyone votes: is a team with a free slot and two injured men blocked? Is the concentration disqualifying, or is “the rule bites hardest on whoever had bad luck” what an injury rule is for? Is roster pressure part of the skill of the game? And does the decay argue for four slots, or for leaving it alone?
