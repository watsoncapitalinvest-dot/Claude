Two Slots, Seven Short

Seven of 16 teams have hit the IR cap with a man still stranded; nine never have. A third slot would clear 62% of it, the wire holds a handful of men worth adding, and in six weeks the cap has cost one side one player — and 7% of 161 comparable leagues run two slots.

The short version
  1. The cap binds, and harder every week. 12 of 16 rosters are full and 18 injured men are sitting on active roster spots. Both numbers have risen every week of the season.
  2. A third slot clears 62% of it — 21 blocked team-weeks become 8 — and frees 4.0 roster spots a week out of 304.
  3. The measurable cost so far is one player. Two drops were forced by a full injured reserve in six weeks, and one of those was a man signed and cut inside a fortnight. No other side has claimed them since.
  4. It does not fall on everyone. Seven of 16 sides have ever been blocked; nine never have. n.W.o alone carry 29% of it.
  5. Two slots is not normal for a league this shape. The median sixteen-team dynasty league runs 4 and 89% run three or more, which puts this league in the bottom 11% of 161. Going to three would still leave it in the bottom 6% on total stash room.
  6. The wire cannot absorb it. Across the five weeks measured, only 2 to 5 unrostered men a week would have improved anybody’s lineup, against 1 to 5 sides who would have taken up a third slot in those same weeks.

The league: 16 teams, 16 owners, one each. 10 starters and nine bench on every roster, plus two injured reserve slots — 304 active spots and 32 IR spots in play. Data through week 6; 96 team-weeks.

1Does the cap bind?

It does, and it has tightened every week of the season. By week 6 12 of 16 rosters were full with 18 injured men stuck on active spots.

Over six weeks, 7 of 16 teams have been blocked at least once, across 21 of 96 team-weeks (22%).

Stranded in week 6 include Will Shipley, Terrance Ferguson, Kaytron Allen, James Conner, Caleb Douglas, Marcus Mariota, Jadarian Price, Ja'Kobi Lane.

Blocked means a side whose injured reserve is full and who still has an injured man on the active roster — the strict reading, used everywhere on this page. A team holding a free slot is not blocked by the cap, whatever else is wrong with its week. The looser reading — which also counts a side with one slot free and two men hurt — would add three team-weeks to every blocked figure on this page (24 of 96 rather than 21). The strict reading was settled on, and it is the one that counts fewer.

2What a third slot would do

Clear 62% of the blockage and free 4.0 roster spots a week — about 1% of the 304 in play.

The counterfactual holds the injuries exactly as they fell and re-runs each week under a different cap, assuming every manager fills IR to the cap with whoever is stranded and changes nothing else. That assumption is the model’s softest joint; it is flagged again in section 8.

A fourth slot is not modelled anywhere on this page and is not on the table. The question in front of the league is two or three, and a counterfactual nobody will vote on only gives the argument somewhere else to go.

Who gains. Only eight sides gain anything at all, and their median seed is 9.5 against a league median of 8.5 — too close to call it a tilt either way.

3Has the cap actually cost anyone a player?

Once. In 367 completed transactions and 284 drops, two sides cut a man they were already carrying injured with no slot left to shelve him.

This is the test the first draft of this paper named as the strongest evidence nobody had. Everything else measured here is friction — a roster slot doing nothing — and friction is a ceiling on inconvenience, not a measure of loss. A drop is the first thing in the argument that is a real cost.

How it is judged. Not by looking up the dropped man’s injury status: Sleeper carries today’s, so a man dropped hurt in September who has since healed reads healthy now. Instead every drop is matched to the IR snapshot taken before it, by timestamp — waivers clear within hours of the Wednesday reading and a week label cannot order the two. Of 284 drops, 116 fall after a snapshot and can be judged; 168 cannot, almost all of them the preseason flood that predates the first reading.

And the size of it. One of two does not survive inspection: Dont'e Thornton was signed and cut inside a fortnight, which is a flyer rather than an asset a manager was holding on to. That leaves one genuine instance in six weeks: Gumbas letting Andrei Iosivas go. Not one of them has been claimed by any of the other 15 teams since, which is the league agreeing by inaction about what the loss was worth.

The comparison this paper asked for, both ways round: 37 stranded man-weeks at a full injured reserve, of which 2 ended in a drop; 27 with a slot free, of which 0 did. The direction is what the expansion case predicts. The sample is nowhere near large enough to carry it, and this paper does not claim otherwise.

4Is it a level playing field?

Equal in form, unequal in effect. Nine of 16 rosters have never once been blocked, and 29% of all the blockage there has been sits on n.W.o.

That cuts against expanding, and it should be said plainly. The cap is not pressure applied to sixteen teams. It is pressure applied to seven, and 29% of it to one. A third slot does not spread the burden evenly; it lifts it where it already falls and leaves the other nine rosters exactly as they were. A change that moves nothing for 56% of the league is a hard thing to call a levelling.

What runs the other way is why the bite is uneven. It tracks nothing a manager chooses. Every roster is built to the same 19 men and the same two slots; what separates n.W.o from the nine untouched sides is which of their players got hurt, and when. Roster pressure that follows from a decision is skill. Roster pressure that follows from one treatment room is not a decision anybody made.

The data settles the shape and not the principle. A third slot would have cleared three of the seven affected teams completely and left four still blocked in at least one week. Whether that is levelling a field or subsidising bad luck is a judgement, and this page does not have the standing to make it.

5What every other 16-team league runs

The median 16-team dynasty league runs 4 slots. 89% run three or more. Twelve of 161 run two, as we do.

This was the one claim in the argument nobody could test. Sleeper has no search — you can ask it for a league by its id, or for the leagues a given manager is in, and nothing else — so the sample is walked outward from here: every manager in this league, every other 2026 league they play in, and onward through theirs. 8,857 leagues from 806 managers, three hops. Of those, 218 have sixteen teams and 161 are sixteen-team dynasty leagues.

Narrowing to the closest match — sixteen-team superflex dynasty, 78 leagues — moves it very little: median 4, 83% at three or more, and this league in the bottom 17%.

Eligibility does not explain it, which was the first place to look. A raw slot count is not comparable: a league with five slots that admits only true injured reserve is stricter than one with three that admits Out, Doubtful and PUP. This league admits two of six designations. Among the 35 sixteen-team dynasty leagues that are exactly as restrictive, the median is still 4 slots, and only two of 35 sit at two or fewer. Across the sample the permissive leagues carry more slots rather than fewer, so eligibility compounds the gap instead of accounting for it.

And injured reserve is not the only place a dynasty roster parks a man. A taxi squad does much the same job for different reasons, and 147 of these 161 leagues have one where this league has none.

Which cuts both ways, and the second way is the one worth noticing. Going to three slots would move this league’s total stash from 2 to 3 against a median of 9 — still the bottom 6%. The distance between this league and its peers is not one injured reserve slot. It is 13 roster spots.

Are they just younger? It was the first objection raised to this section and it is half right. The oldest league in the whole sixteen-team set has run 9 seasons on Sleeper, and it cannot show more, because Sleeper is younger than this league — whose own record came off ESPN before the move. So nobody here has existed as long as the SCFL, and this measurement is a floor for every league in it, including ours at two seasons on the platform.

What the measurement can do is test the reasoning: a league being new is only grounds to discount it if new leagues choose differently. They do, and in the opposite direction.

The longest-running cohort is the least friendly to the case for holding at two: of the 54 sixteen-team dynasty leagues with six or more seasons on Sleeper, only two run two slots or fewer. Their median is 5 slots, 5 taxi and a bench of 17.

Read it as convention, not consequence. Every other section of this paper measures what the cap does. This one measures what other people chose, which is a weaker kind of evidence and a fair thing to set aside on principle. It does settle one thing: two slots is not normal for a league of this shape, and it is not normal among the oldest leagues either. Whether normal is the same as right is not a question any survey answers. The correlation above is also likely confounded — long-tenured leagues on Sleeper are simply deeper leagues, bench 17 against our 9, so this may be "big-roster leagues migrated early" rather than "experience teaches you to add slots".

6The argument against: can the wire absorb it?

No. In the five weeks measured the entire waiver pool held 2 to 5 men a week worth starting, against 1 to 5 sides who would have taken up a third slot in those same weeks.

The mechanism is arithmetic, not opinion. A side that shelves an injured man frees an active spot, fills it, and the pool is one player smaller. Every extra slot used takes a player off waivers.

A headcount cannot test that, and this study does not report one. Counting unrostered bodies gives a number near five hundred all season, which is true and useless — it counts third-string tight ends and quarterbacks nobody in this league would start in any week of any year. The question is how many men are worth adding, so that is what is measured, against a bar the league set itself: did he outscore the median man actually started at his position that week?

In weeks 3, 4 and 5, a third slot would have been claimed by as many sides as there were players on the entire wire worth claiming — and in 4 and 5, by more. Expansion would not thin the usable wire in a week like those. It would empty it.

And the league is quiet, which is the same finding from the other end. Stripping out failed waiver bids, trades and commissioner moves, in-season activity runs 26 completed add-or-drop moves a week across 16 teams — 1.6 per side — with only 9 to 13 of 16 rosters touched in a given week. Managers are not passing up a deep wire. There is not much on it to pass up.

What runs the other way is that the withdrawal is a loan. A player coming off injured reserve has to be activated, the roster is then one over, and somebody is dropped. The men come back. What a third slot changes is not how many players are owned across a season but when — and it takes them in exactly the weeks when injuries make everyone want them.

7The scoreboard

Every claim either side made, and what the data does to it.

Supported means the data backs it. Contradicted means the data runs against it. Overtaken means it was true when it was said and is not now. Untested means this study cannot speak to it.

Of the seven arguments for keeping the cap, one did not survive contact with the data and five did, with one the data cannot speak to. The two that matter most are the two nobody can settle from here: that the wire is thin, which was true before this debate started, and that no competitive harm has been demonstrated — which survived even after the transaction log was pulled looking for exactly that.

9What this cannot tell you

Read this before quoting any figure above.

It measures roster friction, not competitive harm.
Nothing here shows a blocked team lost a game, or even lost points. Establishing that would need to know who each manager would have added with the freed slot and what that man then scored, which is unobservable. Every number in sections 1 and 2 is a ceiling on inconvenience.
A blocked team is never actually stuck.
Any side can cut an injured man and move on — which is exactly what section 3 counts. Blocked means made to choose, not prevented, and whether being made to choose is a flaw or the point of a roster limit is a judgement no amount of this data settles.
The benefit is concentrated, and that is not by itself an objection.
n.W.o account for 6 of the 24 freed spots (25%). An injury rule biting hardest on whoever got hurt most is the rule doing its job, not a distortion in the sample. What it does mean is narrower: the headline percentages rest heavily on one roster, so take that team out and the measured size of the effect falls sharply.
The drop test can only undercount.
A snapshot is weekly. A man signed on Thursday, hurt on Sunday and dropped on Tuesday was never in one, so he cannot be counted either way. Those drops are reported as unobserved rather than quietly scored as healthy — but it means section 3 is a floor, not a total.
The peer survey is a snowball sample, not a random one.
Sleeper has no search, so section 5 is walked outward from this league. That draws on people who already play in a sixteen-team superflex dynasty league, so it skews toward deeper and more committed leagues than the true population of sixteen-team dynasty leagues. The gap it measures is therefore probably overstated. It also measures tenure in Sleeper seasons only, which is a floor for any league that migrated — this one included.
One season, six weeks, 96 team-weeks.
Injury rates vary year to year and no claim is made that this one is typical. The greedy-filling assumption in the model is untested — a manager might hold a slot open against a worse injury later, in which case uptake above is overstated.

The relief looks weaker later in the season, but the trend is noisy. The share of each week’s blockage a third slot would clear has run 100%, 100%, 75%, 80%, 25%, 50% — averaging 92% over the first three weeks and 52% since. It is not a steady decline: the worst week was 5, at 25%, and week 6 reads 50% (3 of 6 blocked sides cleared). The mechanism is real — a side with more than one man stranded only has its line moved by a single extra slot — but on six weeks it cannot be called a trend, and it is not monotonic. Stranded counts have run 3, 5, 10, 13, 15, 18, so the problem is growing even where the relief is holding up.
