Two Slots, Six Short

Six of 16 teams have been an IR slot short this season. A third slot would clear 69% of it — and here is everything that measurement cannot tell you.

This is a working paper, not a proposal. It measures how often the two-slot injured reserve cap actually binds, and how much of that a third slot would relieve, using the league’s own weekly roster data. It does not argue that anybody lost a game because of the cap — that claim is not testable with this data, and nothing here should be read as making it.

The desk has a position in the debate. The definitions, the counterfactual and the source files are set out in full so the position can be put aside and the work checked.

Where the numbers come from

ir-watch.json — one row per week, written by scripts/ir_watch.py from the Sleeper API at the time of that week, and never rewritten afterwards. Every figure below was recorded live, so none of it benefits from hindsight about who got hurt later.

Injured means Sleeper carries one of IR, Out, Doubtful, PUP, Sus, NA, COV, DNR. Questionable is not counted — including week-to-week knocks would inflate every number here. Stranded means an injured man occupying an active roster spot: he is the unit of harm in this study, a roster slot doing nothing.

Blocked means a team that cannot shelve everyone it would want to. There are two defensible readings, and they disagree on exactly one team-week of 80: whether a side with a free slot and more injured men than slots counts as blocked. This page counts it, on the grounds that a team a slot short is a slot short. A reader who prefers the stricter reading should subtract it.

Does the cap bind?

By week 5, 9 of 16 rosters are full and 15 injured players occupy active roster spots league-wide. Over five weeks, 6 of 16 teams have been a slot short at least once, across 16 of 80 team-weeks (20%).

Stranded in week 5 include Caleb Williams, Terrance Ferguson, Jalen Coker, Pat Bryant, Dont'e Thornton, Tank Dell, Jadarian Price, Tank Bigsby.

What a third slot would have done

The counterfactual holds the injuries exactly as they fell and re-runs each week under a different cap, assuming every manager fills IR up to the cap with whoever is stranded and changes nothing else. That assumption is the model’s softest joint and is flagged again below.

Going from two slots to three removes 69% of the blocked team-weeks and 62% of the stranded player-weeks. Going to four removes 81% — the second extra slot buys roughly a fraction of what the first one does. If a line is to be drawn, three is where the curve bends.

The magnitude is small. 16 roster spots freed across five weeks — 3.2 a week, against 304 active roster spots in a 16-team, 19-man league. About 1%.

Who it helps

Median seed of the teams helped: 11.0, against a league median of 8.5. On this sample the change helps the bottom of the table more than the top.

Limitations

Read this before quoting any figure above.

It measures roster friction, not competitive harm. Nothing here shows a blocked team lost a game, or even lost points. Establishing that would need to know who each manager would have added with the freed slot and what that man then scored, which is unobservable. Every number above is a ceiling on inconvenience.

A blocked team is never actually stuck. There were 503 free agents in week 1. Any side can cut an injured man and move on. Blocked means made to choose, not prevented — and whether being made to choose is a flaw or the point of a roster limit is a judgement no amount of this data settles.

The benefit is concentrated. n.W.o account for 5 of the 16 freed spots (31%) and were a slot short in five of five weeks. A fair reader may ask whether a permanent rule change is warranted by one roster’s injury luck. Take that team out and the case weakens materially.

The relief decays as the season goes on. In weeks 1 and 2, a third slot resolved the problem completely. By week 5 it clears only 1 of 4 blocked teams, because the worst-affected sides now have more than one man stranded and a single extra slot merely moves their line. Stranded counts have run 3, 5, 10, 13, 15. If that continues, a third slot relieves a shrinking share of a growing problem, and this page is quoting its own best case.

One season, five weeks, 80 team-weeks. Injury rates vary year to year and no claim is made that this one is typical. The greedy-filling assumption in the model is untested — a manager might hold a slot open against a worse injury later, in which case uptake above is overstated. And transactions are not observed, so we cannot see whether any blocked side actually cut a player it wanted to keep. That would be the strongest evidence of real cost, and we do not have it.

What would settle it

Transaction logs. Drops of injured players by teams at the cap against drops by teams with a slot free. If the cap forces real losses, it shows there. More weeks. The decay above is the open question, and re-running this at week 10 and week 14 answers it. Earlier seasons. The collector only began logging this year, so 2026 is all there is.

Four questions worth answering before anyone votes: is a team with a free slot and two injured men blocked? Is the concentration disqualifying, or is “the rule bites hardest on whoever had bad luck” what an injury rule is for? Is roster pressure part of the skill of the game? And does the decay argue for four slots, or for leaving it alone?
