Two Slots, Seven Short

Seven of 16 teams have hit the IR cap with a man still stranded this season; nine never have. A third slot would clear 62% of it — and here is everything that measurement cannot tell you.

The league: 16 teams, 16 owners, one each. 10 starters and nine bench on every roster, plus two injured reserve slots — 304 active spots and 32 IR spots in play across the league.

This is a working paper, not a proposal. It measures how often the two-slot injured reserve cap actually binds, and how much of that a third slot would relieve, using the league’s own weekly roster data. It does not argue that anybody lost a game because of the cap — that claim is not testable with this data, and nothing here should be read as making it.

The desk has a position in the debate. The definitions, the counterfactual and the source files are set out in full so the position can be put aside and the work checked.

Where the numbers come from

ir-watch.json — one row per week, written by scripts/ir_watch.py from the Sleeper API at the time of that week, and never rewritten afterwards. Every figure below was recorded live, so none of it benefits from hindsight about who got hurt later.

Injured means Sleeper carries one of IR, Out, Doubtful, PUP, Sus, NA, COV, DNR. Questionable is not counted — including week-to-week knocks would inflate every number here. Stranded means an injured man occupying an active roster spot: he is the unit of harm in this study, a roster slot doing nothing.

Blocked means a side whose injured reserve is full and who still has an injured man on the active roster. That is the strict reading, and it is the one this page uses throughout: a team holding a free slot is not blocked by the cap, whatever else is wrong with its week. An earlier draft left the choice open. It is closed now, and closed the way that counts fewer blocked weeks — the looser reading, which counts a side with one slot free and two men hurt, would add three team-weeks to every blocked figure below (24 of 96 rather than 21).

Does the cap bind?

By week 6, 12 of 16 rosters are full and 18 injured players occupy active roster spots league-wide. Over six weeks, 7 of 16 teams have been blocked at least once, across 21 of 96 team-weeks (22%).

Stranded in week 6 include Will Shipley, Terrance Ferguson, Kaytron Allen, James Conner, Caleb Douglas, Marcus Mariota, Jadarian Price, Ja'Kobi Lane.

What a third slot would have done

The counterfactual holds the injuries exactly as they fell and re-runs each week under a different cap, assuming every manager fills IR up to the cap with whoever is stranded and changes nothing else. That assumption is the model’s softest joint and is flagged again below.

Going from two slots to three removes 62% of the blocked team-weeks and 57% of the stranded player-weeks. A fourth slot is not modelled anywhere on this page and is not on the table: the question in front of the league is two or three, and a counterfactual nobody is going to vote on only gives the argument somewhere else to go.

The magnitude is small. 24 roster spots freed across six weeks — 4.0 a week, against 304 active roster spots in a 16-team, 19-man league. About 1%.

Who it helps

The two right-hand columns do not measure the same thing, and one row shows it: Killer Klowns gains a slot without ever having been blocked. Weeks blocked is the strict count — injured reserve full, a man still stranded. Spots gained is simply where a third slot would have been filled, which includes a side holding one slot free and two men hurt. Uptake is wider than blockage, and the wire section below needs the wider figure because every slot filled takes a player.

Median seed of the teams helped: 9.5, against a league median of 8.5. On this sample the change helps the bottom of the table more than the top.

Is it a level playing field?

Every roster in the league carries the same two slots, so the rule is equal in form. It is not equal in effect. Of 16 teams, 7 have been blocked at least once and nine never have — not once in six weeks. n.W.o alone carry 6 of the 21 blocked team-weeks (29%).

That cuts against the case for expanding, and it should be said plainly. The cap is not pressure applied to sixteen teams. It is pressure applied to seven, and 29% of it to one. A third slot does not spread the burden evenly; it lifts it where it already falls and leaves the other nine rosters exactly as they were. On a strict reading of “level playing field”, a change that moves nothing for 56% of the league is not a levelling.

What runs the other way is why the bite is uneven. It does not track anything a manager chooses. Every roster is built to the same 19 men and the same two slots; what separates n.W.o from the nine untouched sides is which of their players got hurt, and in what order. Roster pressure that follows from a decision is skill. Roster pressure that follows from n.W.o’s treatment room is not a decision anyone made.

The data settles the shape and not the principle. A third slot would have cleared three of the seven affected teams completely and left four still blocked in at least one week. Whether that is levelling a field or subsidising bad luck is a judgement, and this page does not have the standing to make it.

The argument against: does it thin the wire?

The case against expansion is that the wire is already bare and another slot makes it barer. The mechanism is arithmetic, not opinion: a side that shelves an injured man frees an active spot, fills it, and the pool is one player smaller. Every extra slot used takes a player off waivers.

A headcount cannot test that, and this study does not report one. Counting unrostered bodies gives a number around five hundred all season, which is true and useless — it counts third-string tight ends and quarterbacks nobody in this league would start in any week of any year. The question is how many men are worth adding, so that is what is measured.

Two bars, both set by what the league itself started that week. Would upgrade a lineup means he outscored the median man started at his position — the players managers actually chase. Beats replacement means he outscored the worst man started at his position, which is a much weaker test.

The entire waiver wire holds 2 to 5 players a week who would improve anybody’s starting lineup. In week 5 there were 3 — K.Williams WR, T.Quitoriano TE, I.Bond WR. A third slot would have been taken up by 4 teams that same week, and those teams take the best man available, not the fourth best.

In week 5 a third slot would have been claimed by four teams, and there were three players on the entire wire worth claiming. Expansion does not thin the usable wire. In a week like that one it empties it.

And the demand is rising: by week 6, 8 teams would have used a third slot. Week 6 has not been played, so it has no depth figure yet — but no week measured so far has offered more than 5 players worth having.

What runs the other way is that the withdrawal is a loan. A player coming off injured reserve has to be activated, the roster is then one over, and somebody is dropped. The men come back. What a third slot changes is not how many players are owned across a season but when they are owned — and it takes them in exactly the weeks when injuries make everyone want them.

The scoreboard

Every claim either side made in the argument of 7 September, and what six weeks of data does to it. Supported means the data backs it. Contradicted means the data runs against it. Overtaken means it was true when it was said and is not now. Untested means this study cannot speak to it.

Where it lands

On the count it leans neither way — dead level. Of the five arguments made for keeping the cap, one did not survive contact with the data and three did, with one the data cannot speak to. The ones that stood are the ones nobody can settle from here: that the wire is thin, which was true before this debate started, and that no competitive harm has ever been demonstrated, which is true and may stay true.

The strongest surviving argument on either side is not about fairness or hoarding. It is that a third slot takes its players off the top of a wire that holds barely a dozen usable men — and takes them in exactly the weeks when injuries make everyone want them.

Limitations

Read this before quoting any figure above.

It measures roster friction, not competitive harm. Nothing here shows a blocked team lost a game, or even lost points. Establishing that would need to know who each manager would have added with the freed slot and what that man then scored, which is unobservable. Every number above is a ceiling on inconvenience.

A blocked team is never actually stuck. Any side can cut an injured man and move on. Blocked means made to choose, not prevented — and whether being made to choose is a flaw or the point of a roster limit is a judgement no amount of this data settles.

The benefit is concentrated, and that is not by itself an objection. n.W.o account for 6 of the 24 freed spots (25%) and were blocked in six of six weeks. An earlier draft of this paper put that forward as a reason for caution — whether a permanent rule change is warranted by one roster’s injury luck. The answer the desk now takes is that an injury rule biting hardest on whoever got hurt most is the rule doing its job, not a distortion in the sample. What the concentration does mean is narrower and still worth knowing: the headline percentages above rest heavily on one roster, so take that team out and the measured size of the effect falls sharply even if the case does not.

The relief looks weaker later in the season, but the trend is noisy. The share of each week’s blockage a third slot would clear has run 100%, 100%, 75%, 80%, 25%, 50% — averaging 92% over the first three weeks and 52% since. It is not a steady decline: the worst week was 5, at 25%, and week 6 recovered to 50% (3 of 6 blocked teams cleared). The mechanism is real — a side with more than one man stranded only has its line moved by a single extra slot — but on six weeks it cannot be called a trend, and it is not monotonic. Stranded counts have run 3, 5, 10, 13, 15, 18, so the problem is certainly growing even where the relief is holding up.

One season, six weeks, 96 team-weeks. Injury rates vary year to year and no claim is made that this one is typical. The greedy-filling assumption in the model is untested — a manager might hold a slot open against a worse injury later, in which case uptake above is overstated. And transactions are not observed, so we cannot see whether any blocked side actually cut a player it wanted to keep. That would be the strongest evidence of real cost, and we do not have it.

What would settle it

Transaction logs. Drops of injured players by teams at the cap against drops by teams with a slot free. If the cap forces real losses, it shows there. More weeks. Whether the relief holds up as injuries pile in is the open question above, and re-running this at week 10 and week 14 answers it. Earlier seasons. The collector only began logging this year, so 2026 is all there is.

The questions, answered

Which leaves one question the data cannot answer and the league will have to. The rule is the same two slots for everybody and it has bitten seven sides in six weeks. Is a cap that nine rosters have never once noticed a level playing field, or the absence of one?
