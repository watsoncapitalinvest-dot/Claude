The Ledger · A Trade Audit
WEST PALM BEACH, FLA.

Not Guilty of Cheating. Guilty of Being Good.

For twenty-one years the league has whispered that The Machines rig it. We pulled every trade in the dynasty ledger and priced every asset at the season it moved in. Here is what it actually says.

There is a theory of the Skirt Chasers Fantasy League that has survived longer than most of the franchises in it. The theory is simple and it is repeated, in some form, every single autumn: Keith cheats. The Machines are the Evil Empire. He preys on the weak owners, he collects the rebuilders like toll booths, and the rest of the league is left to split the scraps he does not want. It is a good story. It has a villain, it has victims, and it explains — cleanly, comfortably — why the same team keeps ending up on top. This newsroom decided to do the one thing nobody in twenty-one years of group chat had bothered to do. We pulled the receipts.

The record itself is not in dispute, and it is the reason the theory exists at all. The Machines are 215-63 across the tracked era — a .773 winning percentage in a league where the next best side to have gone the full twenty-one seasons, The Beaver Eaters, sits on .590. Twenty playoff appearances in twenty-one seasons. Four championships, nine runner-up finishes, an all-play record (3,198-944-28) that reads like a typo. You do not have to like it to respect it, and most of the league has chosen the first option. When a team is that good for that long, the human brain does not reach for "well-run." It reaches for "rigged." That instinct is the entire case for the prosecution, and it is worth taking seriously — so we did.

The charge, stated plainly, is collusion: that Keith wins because a handful of soft, compliant owners feed him their best players for pennies. If that were true, it would leave a fingerprint. Collusion is narrow by nature — you cannot conspire with the whole room. So we mapped every trade in the itemised ledger, 256 deals from 2021 to 2026, which is the dynasty era and all of it; there was no dynasty market before that and so there is nothing before that to read. Sixty-seven of those deals have The Machines on one side of the table.

The first thing the ledger does is refuse to clear him. His most frequent partner is Lil Chops, with fifteen deals. Second is Wookie Leaks, with fourteen. Between them that is twenty-nine of his sixty-seven deals — about 43 per cent of everything he has done — and both of them come from the bottom of the all-time table. Wookie Leaks have the worst winning percentage of any side still in this league, 51-97, and have never made the playoffs in eleven seasons. Lil Chops are third-worst of the sixteen, at 40-56. If you wanted to draw the prosecution case as a chart, that is the chart. We are not going to pretend otherwise, because it is the first thing in the file.

The second thing the ledger does is refuse to convict him. Those sixty-seven deals are spread across fourteen different franchises — effectively everyone who has held a roster in the dynasty era. Twenty-seven of them were with sides that have made the playoffs ten times or more. Twenty-six were with sides that have won the thing. His joint-second most frequent partner is Powers of Pain, who own five championships — more than Keith does — and who have done eight deals with him. That is not a cabal of three. That is a man doing business with the entire building, including the parts of it that can beat him.

Both things are true at once, and the theory cannot hold both. Collusion needs a closed room. What the ledger shows is an open one with two doors that get used more than the others — and in a dynasty league, those two doors have a name. The rebuilders are not the contender's victims; they are the contender's trading partners by design. A team going nowhere in November has one asset a title team wants and one thing it needs, and the title team is the only reliable buyer in the building. The sides who trade with Keith most are the sides with the most reason to.

And the direction of travel is the opposite of the one the story tells. Across those sixty-seven deals The Machines took in fifty-two players and gave up seventy-four. They took in fifty-four picks and gave up forty-three. By the count, the Evil Empire is a net seller of players and a net buyer of draft capital — which is not what hoovering up the league's veterans looks like. It is what a rebuild looks like, run every year, by a team that keeps winning while it runs it.

He is not even the busiest man in the market. Pork Chop Express appear in seventy-two deals in this ledger; The Machines appear in sixty-seven. And the rate never moves. Thirteen trades in 2021, 2022, 2023, 2024 and 2025: five straight seasons on the same number, with a different set of partners every time. Whatever he is doing, he is doing it in public, on a schedule, with everyone.

Does he win the deals? More than he loses, and by less than the theory needs. Priced at the season each trade was made in — August values of that year, picks on the same dollar board the Trade Court grades on, no hindsight anywhere — The Machines are 16-7 across the sixty-six two-team deals in the ledger. But the number that says more is the third one: forty-three of those sixty-six finish inside twelve dollars either way. 65 per cent of everything he has done is, by the board, a fair trade. His median deal is worth $3 to him. That is the shape of a man making a great many ordinary trades, not a man running a protection racket.

And the misses are not abstract. Some of them walked back through the door. JuJu Smith-Schuster went to Lil Chops in 2021 and was bought back from Heavy Hitters in 2024, three years later. Trey McBride went to Killer Klowns in 2023 and was bought back from Lil Chops in 2025, two years later. Five players in this ledger have made that round trip. It is not the paperwork of a man with the game in a fix. It is the paperwork of a very good manager who is still, occasionally, human — which is the most exculpatory evidence we found.

So why does the accusation never die? Because it does useful work for the fifteen other guys. "Keith cheats" is a more bearable explanation than "Keith is better than me at the one thing we all show up to do." The persecution complex Keith wears — everyone's teaming up on me, they gang up because I'm the best — is not entirely paranoia; the chat does organize against him, does root against him, does treat a deal that helps The Machines as a threat to the republic. But that is a tax the league levies on excellence, not evidence of a crime. They gang up on him because he keeps winning. He keeps winning because he is good.

This newsroom has now read every deal in the dynasty ledger, priced every asset at the season it moved in, and counted every partner. There is no cabal. There is a concentration, and an explanation for the concentration that the concentration itself supports. There is a market, open every summer, and he does not own it: Pork Chop Express appear in more of its deals than he does, and he does not clear it either, because most of what he does in it comes out level. And there is a room that has decided, for twenty-one years, that whatever he is doing instead must be against the rules.

So the verdict of the ledger is not close, and it is not the one the league wants either. On the charge of cheating: not guilty. On the charge of collusion: not guilty. On the charge of running a market that pays him and nobody else: not guilty — forty-three of his sixty-six priced deals come out level, and he is a net seller of players in the ones that do not. He does not have the most championships either; Powers of Pain have five to his four. What he is guilty of is narrower than the theory and worse for everybody else: the best record anybody has put together here, .773 against .590 for the next side to go the distance, and twenty playoff appearances in twenty-one seasons, three more than anyone else has managed. He is beaten in his own trade ledger seven times out of sixty-six and he finishes above you anyway. Some empires are built in the dark. This one was built in the group chat, in broad daylight, one ordinary deal at a time, while everybody watched and nobody could stop it.

How This Was Counted

Every figure in this piece is computed from three files in the newsroom repository, and the piece is regenerated from them rather than typed. The first version of it was typed, and almost every count in the paragraph carrying the defence was wrong.

The ledger. The itemised Obvibase export: 256 deals from 2021 to 2026, with multi-team legs consolidated and pass-through assets netted out, so a middleman is credited only with what he kept. There is nothing before 2021; there was no dynasty market, and the league WhatsApp — created in 2016 — holds nothing but three setup notices until 2021 either. Both records start in the same place, which is why this is the dynasty-era ledger and not the twenty-one-year one.

Players are priced from the DynastyProcess superflex board, taken from the August snapshot of the season the trade was made in. Not today’s. A player who became a star in 2024 is priced in a 2021 trade at what he was worth in 2021, because the question is whether the deal was fair when it was made, not whether it aged well.

Picks are priced from the league’s own pick curve, calibrated against 235 real SCFL trades. A pick with a known slot is worth what the curve says. A future pick has no slot yet, so it is priced at the average of its round and discounted for every year of waiting.

The two boards had to be reconciled, and that is where this audit nearly went wrong. The pick curve puts the 1.01 at 1,000. The player board puts the 1.01 at 100 and the best player in the league at 127. They are the same scale ten units apart, and the first run of this audit added them together raw — which valued a pick three drafts away at nine times a top-five tight end and inflated every pick in the ledger about tenfold. Dividing the curve by ten puts the 1.01 on $100 and the average first-round pick on $45.60, which the league’s Trade Court, calibrated separately, independently puts at $48. Two boards built apart agreeing inside five per cent is the check that this is a reconciliation and not a convenience.

A win is a net above $12 on that board, which is the Trade Court’s own threshold, and anything inside it is called level. What is left out: five three-team deals, whose receipts do not say which side each asset came from, and seven assets league-wide — team defences, mostly — that carry no price and count as zero.

What is deliberately not here. No league ranking for who trades best. The order moves by several places depending on nothing more than what an unknown future second is assumed to be worth, and a table an assumption can reshuffle is not a finding. No claim about how a trade aged, either: this board does not know, and neither did anybody at the table.
