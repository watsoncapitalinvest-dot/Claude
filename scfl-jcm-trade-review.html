The Hitters Paid a First for a Second-Round Running Back

Heavy Hitters receive
RB Jacory Croskey-Merritt
Pork Chop Express receives
2027 1st · late

The Heavy Hitters have bought a running back, and on the surface it is a modest piece of business: a late first-round pick, the cheapest first they own, for a twenty-five-year-old starter coming off a thousand-yard rookie season. The Pork Chop Express did not have to think about it for very long. That is the tell.

Bad trade for the Heavy Hitters, and a clean win for the Express. Not because Croskey-Merritt is a bad player — because the price was a full round too high, paid in the wrong currency, out of the wrong draft year.

That is the whole review in one line. In our own system Croskey-Merritt prices out at exactly a mid-second-round pick. The Heavy Hitters paid a first. That is a four-to-one overpay before anyone argues about upside — and his number moved from 0 to 3 over the last twelve months, so the breakout was real, and it still did not carry him into first-round territory.

What the market says

The public boards agree, which is the part that should worry the Hitters. KeepTradeCut has him RB37, Tier 12. Other trackers put him around 141st overall and RB39, or 95th and RB37 depending on their weighting. There is no dynasty market anywhere that prices him inside a first-round pick.

The pick is worth more than “late” suggests

And here the deal gets worse rather than better. The 2027 class is the one the entire dynasty world is hoarding for: a generational receiver at the top in Jeremiah Smith, and a quarterback group deep enough to matter in a superflex league. KeepTradeCut is currently valuing a late 2027 first above a mid 2026 first. Apply that premium to our board and the pick the Hitters surrendered is not a 13 — it is closer to a 25 or 29. Call the real multiple six to one.

The case for the player

He is not a punchline, and this desk is not going to pretend otherwise. As a rookie he ran for 805 yards and eight touchdowns on 175 carries, led Washington in rushing, and PFF graded him 30th among starting NFL backs. He enters 2026 entrenched as the Commanders’ RB1 in a new outside-zone scheme that suits him, at twenty-five, with a Year 2 leap plausibly in front of him.

The case against is in the same paragraph. He caught nine passes all season. Rachaad White is the better third-down back and will take those snaps. Kaytron Allen and Jerome Ford are in the room competing for the rest. He is a two-down runner in a committee on a team that keeps adding backs — which is precisely why every board in the industry prices him as a second-rounder rather than a first.

The joke, for the archive

None of this should obscure what the Pork Chop Express pulled off. He took Croskey-Merritt 18th in August 2025 and the league laughed. The Powers of Pain graded the class a D — “This draft feels more like a waiver report than actual draft picks” — and wrote that “it is hard to envision him returning value for more than a year or two.” The Machines was blunter: “Crowley Merrit will be working at a summer camp by 2027.”

The pick the Express just collected for him is a 2027 first.

Why the Express sold

Because he said so six days earlier, while lecturing a different franchise about this exact behaviour. “You have to make moves in this league,” he wrote on August 9. “Stagnate is not an option.” And: “When guys get old, you sell to a contender and move on.” He sold a hype cycle at its peak, for the most desirable pick class on the board, from a roster he openly builds by trade rather than draft: “I haven’t drafted anyone in my starting lineup other than Bowers.”

Why the Hitters bought

Because the rebuild is over and they know it. They held the 1.01 in July and spent it on Jeremiyah Love, the player the Powers of Pain called “the only true elite talent in this year’s draft,” asking whether he would be the one to lead them “out of the SCFL basement.” They took Omar Cooper Jr. at 1.09, drew a B+ for the class, and have now gone shopping for a finished player. The instinct is right — a team cannot collect lottery tickets forever.

The execution is the problem. They spent a first on the one position their own league treats as a depreciating asset — “the average running backs career is three seasons,” as The Machines put it, adding that it is “really difficult to get more than four or five good FANTASY seasons out of any running back. We’ve been doing this a long time — the data is there.” They spent it on a one-season sample. And they spent it out of the 2027 class while already holding an elite young back.

Pork Chop Express, A. He converted a mid-second asset into a first-round pick, timed it to the top of the player’s market, and did it out of the draft year that carries a premium. The only quibble is his own standard — “I rarely take the first offer from guys” — which invites the question of what a bidding war might have returned.

SCFL NewsRoom · The Trade Desk · August 2026

Heavy Hitters, C−. Buying players instead of picks is the correct instinct for a team with a 1.01 back and no more excuses, and a late first is the cheapest first they own. They still paid four times the board price — six if the 2027 premium holds — for a two-down back in a four-man committee. What keeps this out of the D range is that the damage is capped: a late 2027 first is a modest asset in absolute terms, and no franchise is set back by losing one. This is not a wound. It is waste.

If Croskey-Merritt takes the Year 2 leap it looks defensible by December. If he splits carries the way the depth chart suggests, the Heavy Hitters have funded a rival’s next rebuild with a pick out of the best class in years.
