How An Issue Is Made

Sixteen teams and four days of real football, rebuilt into eight games you can watch back on a single sixty-minute clock — and then written up off those. How an issue of the Skirt Chasers gets made.

01The week comes in

Sixteen teams, eight matchups, and every point every man scored. Which is where most fantasy leagues stop. You get a column of totals, a winner and a loser, and no idea what actually happened.

A fantasy score is an abstraction — a number that appeared beside a name at some point over four days. Everything that follows exists to turn it back into football.

02Every point is traced back to the play that made it

Each one is matched to the actual thing that happened on a real field: the catch, the run, the sack, the kick. Around 1,578 of them in a normal week. The player, the yardage, the moment it happened, what it was worth.

So a score is no longer a number. It is a list of plays, in the order they were really made.

03The week is rebuilt as eight football games

This is the part worth understanding. Those plays get laid onto a single sixty-minute clock, so a matchup spread across four days becomes one game that runs from a kickoff to a final whistle. A touchdown caught on Monday night becomes a late-fourth-quarter touchdown. Two men who never shared a field are now on the same drive.

And then you can watch it back. Not a summary of it — the game itself, play by play, with the clock running and the score moving and the lead changing hands in front of you. Pick any of the eight and sit through it. A fantasy matchup that nobody watched, because there was never anything to watch, turns into sixty minutes you can follow.

Nothing in it is invented. The plays are real, the yardage is real, the order is real. The clock is the only thing that is made up, and it is made up the same way every week.

04The articles are written off the replay

Which is the whole reason for building it. A report is not written off a column of totals, because you cannot write anything off a column of totals. It is written off the game — who scored, when, what it did to the lead, which of them held. A normal week has about 27 lead changes in it across the eight games, and the replay knows where every one of them is.

That makes a sentence like “he took the lead back with four minutes left and it stayed taken” a thing that is either true or false rather than a thing that sounds good. It is in the record or it is not. Every figure in every sentence is read out of the replay when the page is built, so the number in the prose and the number in the game are the same number by construction.

05A picture of the play that decided it

Each report opens on the moment the game turned — the last time the lead changed in a close one, the biggest single play in a one-sided one — drawn as though it had happened for the fantasy team that owned the player, in that team’s own colours rather than his real club’s. His jersey, his number, his build.

The world around him is the real one, read off the same tape as the play: the right stadium, the right weather, the right hour of the day, and whether the roof was open or shut. If the roof was shut there is no sky in the picture, and if nobody played in rain that week then nothing in the issue is wet.

06The cover

Drawn to a written brief, and the brief has to think about the finished page rather than the picture: a masthead sits across the top and a headline across the bottom, so both of those bands are left deliberately empty before anything is drawn into them.

The cover and the little preview picture that shows up when somebody pastes the link are cut from the same artwork, so the thing you see in the message is the thing you get when you open it.

07It is laid out like a magazine

Not a web page that happens to contain football. Pages, in order, that turn — around 19 of them in an issue, opening on a cover and closing on next Sunday.

Where the text falls is measured rather than guessed. The issue is built, every page is then checked at phone size to find where it actually runs out of room, and it is built a second time against those real breaks. Anything too long carries over as a continuation rather than as a new article, the way a magazine does it.

08It ends on next Sunday

Every issue closes on the week ahead: a paragraph a game, eight of them, so all sixteen clubs get said something about rather than the same three being written about every week. What the fixture is worth, what each side leaves with, and what the two of them have done to each other before.

That last part reaches back 21 seasons — 2005–2025 of results and 107 separate head-to-head records, the most lopsided of which stands at 26–1 over 27 meetings. A fixture that one of them is on the wrong end of is worth something the standings cannot tell you, and the preview is where that gets said.

Each line only prints when the thing it describes is actually true of the fixture in front of it. Which is why there is no “should be a good one” anywhere in it.

The short version: the week gets rebuilt into football, and then the football gets reported. Every sentence in an issue sits on a play that really happened, for points that were really scored, at the point in the game where the rebuilt record puts it — because the sentence and the number in it are both read back out of that record when the page is built. Nothing is written from memory, and nothing that cannot be traced to a play goes in at all.
