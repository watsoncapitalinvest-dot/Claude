01The week arrives

api.sleeper.app is blocked from the machine this desk runs on, so the scores are fetched by a GitHub Action and come back through git. Only one run is kept per group, which is how week 2 was lost once — two dispatches in quick succession cancelled each other.

A half-finished week looks exactly like a finished one, so a week counts as played only on two tells: nobody below 30 points, and the share of starters on exactly zero. A finished week runs 1 to 6 per cent. A Sunday evening reads 10. One tell alone has flipped an article’s conclusion twice.

02The games are rebuilt from the tape

Every fantasy point is matched back to the real NFL play that produced it, joining on the league’s own player id and never on a name. Sleeper stores that id with a leading space. Two running backs called Wilson will ruin an afternoon.

1,493 plays across eight games are then laid onto a single 60-minute clock, so a Monday-night touchdown becomes a late-fourth-quarter touchdown and the week reads as one afternoon. Nothing is invented: the plays, the yardage and the order are real. Only the clock is made up.

Points no play explains — mostly team defence — are carried as flagged residuals so the running score lands on the true final. There were 20 of them. They are never written about.

03The reports get written

Eight game reports, one a game, from the rebuilt tape. 37 lead changes, 160 starting players and every score either side of every one of them are available to the writing, and the figure in a sentence has to be the figure in the file.

That rule is the whole job. Eight separate figures were caught wrong in this issue alone — a score rounded to 85.40 that was 85.36, a touchdown placed at twelve seconds left that happened at 8:14, a play called the last lead change when four more followed it.

04A picture of the play that mattered

Each report opens on the play that decided it — the last lead change in a close game, the biggest single play in a blowout — drawn as though it happened for the fantasy side that owned the man rather than for his NFL club. Eight of them this week, one a game.

The brief is generated, not written. It carries the two clubs’ own colours from a file of 16, the player’s real jersey number and real height and weight, and the real conditions off the tape: the stadium, the sky, the local time of day, whether the roof was shut. It rained in none of week 7’s games, and the first version of this drew rain.

05The cover

Drawn by a separate graphics AI from a plain-text brief. The brief fixes the composition in pixel rows, because a masthead sits across the top of the frame and a headline sits across the bottom, and both are added after the picture exists.

The cover and the link preview are built from the same file by the same command. The week they were allowed to disagree, the link previewed as a chart and opened on a stadium.

06The issue is laid out, and measured

Pagination is measured rather than guessed. The issue is built once, a headless browser renders every page at phone size and records where each one actually fills up, and it is built again against those breaks. A magazine page that scrolls is a bug.

28 pages this week. Anything still long is scaled down, and anything scaled too far runs on to a second page instead — with a continuation line, not a second headline.

07Next Sunday, argued rather than listed

Every issue closes on the week ahead: a paragraph a game, so all sixteen clubs get said something about. It is generated from the table, the week files and 21 seasons of history — 2005–2025 for lifetime records, 107 head-to-head series going back to 2008.

Every line hangs off a condition, so a joke about a rout prints only over a rout. Repeated phrasings are capped, including against the paragraph directly above, because the same sentence twice reads worse than silence.

08Then it has to survive

Saved, filed and sendable, checked by a script before anything is called finished. Saved because this machine gets wiped. Filed into a catalog of 41 pages so it is reachable later. Sendable means a share card that exists — a page without one arrives as a grey rectangle, and the person who sent it cannot see that from their end.

The short version: nothing in it is typed from memory. If a sentence counts, ranks, compares or characterises something, it is computed from a file in the repository, and if it cannot be computed it does not get printed. That is a rule the desk learned the hard way, and it is why the mistakes that do get through tend to be caught before anybody else sees them.
