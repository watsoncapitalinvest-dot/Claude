How An Issue Is Made

01The week comes in

Sixteen teams, eight games, and every point each man scored. The catch is that a half-finished Sunday looks exactly like a finished one — the scores are simply smaller. So a week is only treated as played once two separate signs agree that it is. Trusting one of them has flipped a conclusion twice.

02Every point is traced back to the play that made it

A fantasy score is an abstraction: a number that appeared beside a name. So each one is matched back to the actual thing that happened on a field — the catch, the run, the sack. 1,493 of them this week. Players are matched by the league’s own identity number rather than by name, because two different men called Wilson will quietly ruin an afternoon.

Those plays are then laid out on a single sixty-minute clock, so the whole week reads as one afternoon: a touchdown caught on Monday night becomes a late-fourth-quarter touchdown. Nothing is invented. The plays, the yards and the order they happened in are all real. The clock is the only thing that is made up.

03The games get written up

Eight reports, one a game, written off that rebuilt afternoon — who scored, when, what it did to the lead. 37 lead changes and 160 starting players are on the table to write from.

The rule that makes it work is dull and absolute: every number in a sentence has to be the number in the record. Not roughly it, not what it was rounded to. Eight separate figures were caught wrong in this issue alone — a score written as 85.40 that was 85.36, a touchdown placed with twelve seconds left that happened with eight minutes left, a play described as the last time the lead changed when four more changes came after it.

04A picture of the play that mattered

Each report opens on the moment that decided the game — the last time the lead changed in a close one, the biggest single play in a one-sided one — drawn as though it had happened for the fantasy team that owned the player, in that team’s own colours rather than his real club’s. Eight pictures, one a game.

The man in the picture is really him: his jersey number, his actual height and weight, the kind of build he has. And the world around him is the real one — the right stadium, the right sky, the right time of day, and whether the roof was open or shut. Not one of week 7’s games was played in rain, and the first attempt at this drew rain.

05The cover

Drawn to a written brief, and the brief has to think about the finished page rather than the picture: a masthead sits across the top and a headline across the bottom, so both of those areas have to be left deliberately empty before anything is drawn.

The cover and the little preview picture that appears when the link is shared come out of the same artwork. The one week they were allowed to differ, the link previewed as a chart and then opened on a stadium.

06It is laid out like a magazine

Not a web page that happens to contain football. Pages, in order, that turn. 28 of them this week.

Where the text falls is measured rather than guessed: the issue is built, every page is checked at phone size to see where it actually runs out of room, and then it is built again against those real breaks. A page that scrolls is a mistake. Anything still too long carries on to a second page instead — as a continuation, not as a new article.

07It ends on next Sunday

Every issue closes on the week ahead: a paragraph for each game, so all sixteen clubs get said something about rather than the same three being written about every week.

It is assembled from the table, the season so far and 21 years of history — 2005–2025 of results, and 107 separate head-to-head records going back to 2008. So a preview can tell you that one of these two has beaten the other once in twenty-seven attempts, and that it was a playoff game.

Each line only prints when the thing it describes is actually true, which is why there is no “should be a good one” anywhere in it.

08Then it has to survive

Three things have to be true before an issue counts as finished. It has to be stored somewhere permanent, because the machine it was built on gets wiped. It has to be listed, so it can be found again in a month rather than only through a link that scrolls out of a chat. And it has to look right when it is shared — a page without a preview picture arrives as a grey rectangle, and the person who sent it has no way of knowing.

The short version: nothing in it is written from memory. If a sentence counts something, ranks something, compares two things or says one was the biggest, that claim is worked out from the record every time the page is built — and if it cannot be worked out, it does not go in. It is a dull rule and it is the whole thing. It is why the errors that do happen get found before anybody else reads them.
