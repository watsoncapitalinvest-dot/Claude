The Findings — six years of trades, decoded

Every league tells itself a story about how it trades. The SCFL’s story is that it trades constantly, recklessly, and mostly on vibes. The ledger says otherwise. Behind six years of 2 a.m. “🚨 OFFICIAL TRADE 🚨” posts sits a market with rules — prices that hold, seasons that repeat, and a top of the draft nobody can buy.

All 235 official trades from 2021 through 2026 were pulled from the chat, and every pick and player put on a single dollar scale — the 1.01 = $100. Here is what the numbers say about how the Skirt Chasers really price the game.

The market never sleeps — but it has a clock

The market runs on two peaks: the August draft frenzy — the busiest month of the year at 73 deals, nine of them crammed into a single day in 2021 — and the November deadline (43), with a dead September and a frozen December in between. Line up deals for those two windows and there’s always a partner; try in the lulls and the room goes quiet. One timing wrinkle worth knowing: this year’s spot picks only change hands in July and August, the run-up to the draft. Miss that window and the pick is spent — from there it’s future capital or nothing.

“The top three picks are a closed shop. Everything else — even an elite quarterback — has a price.”

Quarterbacks are the most expensive thing in the building

Superflex bends the entire price curve around the position. Aging Dak Prescott fetched two future firsts. Jared Goff, in his MVP year, cost picks 9 and 25 plus a future first — roughly $88, nearly the price of the 1.01 itself. Even Jimmy Garoppolo, a mid QB1, went for Alvin Kamara and a pick. Only the untouchable top three picks outrank a good quarterback; below them, a startable arm is the priciest thing on the market.

Prices move — anchor to the range, not the box score

The cautionary tale is Cooper Kupp. At his 2022 peak, three first-round picks changed hands to get him; one year and one hamstring later, the same player fetched a single first. He lost nearly two-thirds of his value in twelve months. The floor is just as fixed: a fading veteran, whoever it is, moves for exactly one future second — the market has a set price for a name on the way down.

The blockbusters — and who runs the room

The richest deal on record is AJ Brown for three firsts and a second (November 2023). The 2022 “Wookie Blowup” shipped Kyler Murray, Justin Herbert, Leonard Fournette and Derrick Henry through one multi-team megadeal. And two desks run the market: Pork Chop Express (89 trades) and the New World Order (71) — each in far more deals than any rival, with the Machines (63) a distant third.

What it all adds up to

For a league that runs on chaos, the SCFL prices its assets with startling discipline. The same handful of laws hold across six years and 235 trades: the top of the draft never moves cheap, quarterbacks carry a tax, a player is worth only as much as his last healthy season, and the calendar decides who’s even at the table. None of it was ever written down — it just got discovered and re-discovered every summer by sixteen managers trading mostly on instinct. Now it’s on paper. The next 235 trades will reward whoever reads the rules instead of the vibes. The receipts follow.

Market Laws — how SCFL actually prices assets

The top 3 are a closed shop. Nobody trades out of the top 3 for a bundle of mid-firsts — only for another top pick or a future top-3 / equivalent. Mind the cliff from pick 3 to pick 4.
Players buy picks. The vast majority of pick trades are player-for-pick, not pick-for-pick.
Current mid-1st ≈ a future 1st. A pick in hand this year trades flat for next year’s first (~$40).
A startable QB > any pick outside the top 5. Superflex bends the board around the position.
Late first (13–16) ≈ a solid rookie or rotational depth — real value, but nowhere near top-3 money.
Late 2nd / a fading vet ≈ a future 2nd (~$15). The league’s loose change.

THE SUPERFLEX QB TAX — the single most important price in your league

Because the league starts two quarterbacks, they are the most expensive assets in the SCFL below the top three picks — the ledger proves it. Dak (aging) fetched two future firsts (~$80). Goff in his MVP year cost picks 9 + 25 + a future first (~$88 — nearly the 1.01). Jimmy G — a mid QB1 — still cost Kamara plus a pick (~$50). A startable QB rivals the top of round one; only the untouchable top three sit above it. Any QB-needy team should expect to pay the tax, and pay it early — the price only rises as the league’s startable arms dry up before the draft.

The Fine Print

How the comps were built — and how to read them

There is no official price sheet in the SCFL, so we built one backwards. Every deal has two sides; when a known quantity crosses — a numbered pick, a future first, a player already priced — it puts a number on whatever came the other way. Do that across 235 trades and the unknowns resolve: Godwin fetched pick 9, so Godwin is worth pick 9; Dak fetched two firsts, so Dak is worth two firsts. Chain enough of them together and a whole market falls out of the noise.

The caveats

These are ranges, not gospel. A contender at the deadline overpays; a rebuilder low-balls the same name a month later. Multi-player packages have to be split by hand, so a dollar or two leaks at the seams. And a single trade is one data point — a player who moved once tells you less than one who moved six times. Where the ledger is thin, the position grid on page 4 does the smoothing.

What the table actually tells you

1 · The hierarchy is real. Read top to bottom and the order never wobbles — quarterbacks and elite receivers at the crown, running backs a rung down, tight ends an afterthought the non-PPR scoring guts.

2 · The age cliff is a sheer drop. The same name can lose two-thirds of its value in a calendar year, and every fading veteran — regardless of pedigree — lands on the exact same number: one future second.

3 · Picks and players are one currency. A WR1 and a future first trade flat. That is why, in the SCFL, players buy picks and picks buy players — and why a single dollar scale can price the entire league.

How to Price a Deal

Add up both sides, land it in a band, then apply the adjustments
6The Method

Price a Trade Idea — add up both sides, then apply four adjustments

Within 8% — dead fair, do it 8–18% — a winner, still gets done Over 18% — a fleecing / veto zone

1 · The Superflex QB premium

A QB is worth +15–25% over grid to any team short a starter — and in a 16-team Superflex, several always are. Paying “too much” in raw dollars for a weekly starter isn’t an overpay; it’s the tax.

2 · Timeline tilt

Contenders pay +10–20% for win-now vets; rebuilders discount any player 30+ by 20–40% and pay up for youth and picks. Price the deal from the other owner’s window, not just yours.

3 · Consolidation premium

The single best player in a deal carries +10–15% — one elite roster spot beats two good ones. It’s why Kupp cost three firsts, not two, and why packaging picks to move up always costs a little extra.

4 · Age & role haircuts

Off the grid: RB over 27 −20%, WR over 30 −15%, QB over 34 −10% (SF softens it). Committee/backup roles sit at the bottom of their tier; locked-in every-week roles at the top.

Worked example — the Mahomes/Lamar swap (2026)
Machines give: Mahomes ($100) + pick 6 ($46) = $146
NWO give: Lamar ($100) + pick 20 ($14) = $114
Two elite QBs, near-even — so the $32 pick gap is the whole story: the room paid a full tier to swap Mahomes for the younger Lamar. The youth premium at the game’s most expensive position, laid bare.
Worked example — the QB tax, itemized (Goff, 2024)
Wookies give: pick 9 ($39) + pick 25 ($9) + a future 1st ($40) = $88
→ and get one player: Jared Goff
An MVP-season Superflex QB cost ~$88 — a hair under the 1.01. That is the tax in one line: the best pick in the deal, a second pick, and a future first, all for a single arm.
Worked example — the Kupp round-trip
2022: three firsts (~$135) for peak Kupp
2023: one future 1st + a flier (~$48) for the same man, hurt
A ~64% haircut in twelve months on one player. Price the range, not the name — and never pay peak for a player the injury report has already downgraded.
Worked example — selling a fading vet
A 30-yo WR2, grid ~$36
Nov contender pays +15% → ~$41 · rebuilder pays −30% → ~$25
Same player, a ~$16 swing on who picks up the phone. The ledger shows every fading name (Keenan, M. Williams, Waller) cashing out for a future 2nd — so shop them to a November contender, not a rebuilder.

Cheat Sheet — the conversions to keep in your head

Currency

1.01 = $100 · top-3 = $76–100 (a closed shop) · big cliff after pick 3 · mid-1st ≈ $41 · future 1st ≈ $40 · future 2nd ≈ $15.

Quarterbacks (SF)

Elite QB ≈ the 1.01 (only the top 3 sit above) · a startable QB1 ≈ a top-5 pick or two future firsts · streamer ≈ a mid-1st · backup ≈ a future 2nd.

Skill players (non-PPR)

WR1/RB1 ≈ a future 1st · WR2/RB2 ≈ a late first · any fading vet ≈ a future 2nd · TEs discounted hard — only the top 3 clear $30.

The verdict bands

Within 8% = fair, make it · 8–18% = someone’s winning but it’ll get done · over 18% = a fleece / veto zone. Add the QB tax before calling it an overpay.

The Playbook

Ten rules the ledger hands every SCFL manager
7Advice & Tips

Everything here falls out of the data on the preceding pages. If a manager remembers nothing else from this feature, these are the ten.

🛒 Buying

Pay the QB tax, and pay it early. A startable Superflex arm rivals the top of round one, and the shelf thins as the draft nears — QB-needy teams should buy before the run, not during it.
Never pay peak for a player past peak. Kupp went ~$135 → ~$48 in a year. Buy the dip on names the league has soured on; don’t chase last season’s box score.
Know the ceiling before packaging picks. The top 3 are a closed shop. Two back-half firsts (a 15 & 16, say) top out around pick 10 — and only in a weak class. They can’t buy the top, no matter the bundle.

💰 Selling

Sell fading vets to November contenders, never to rebuilders — same player, a ~$16 swing depending on who picks up the phone.
A fading name = one future 2nd. The league’s universal price for a veteran on the way down. Don’t take less; don’t pay more.
Move spot picks in July, at a premium, before the draft passes and this-year picks vanish from the market entirely.

⏰ Timing

Line deals up for August & November. That’s when the market is open — 73 and 43 deals. September and December are dead air.
Hold future picks as year-round currency. Once the draft passes they’re the only pick that trades — the entire fall runs on them.

⚖ Pricing

Anchor to the range, not the last game. Discount hard for age (RB 27+, WR 30+); the grid is the baseline, the age curve is the swing.
Add both sides in dollars, then land the band. Within 8% is fair, over 18% gets vetoed — then add the roster need on top before calling it an overpay.

THE ONE-LINE VERSION

Buy quarterbacks early, sell old names to contenders in November, price everything off the 1.01 = $100 scale, respect the closed shop at the top of the draft, and never confuse a hot month for a permanent value. The market has rules — now the whole league can see them.
