What A Second-Round Pick Is Actually Worth

This one started as a correction. The desk had been pricing second-round picks like real capital in trade talk, and got told flatly that a mid-second could not possibly be worth that much. It isn't. The league's own chart has been saying so since it was built, and nobody had read it out loud.

The arithmetic

The chart puts the 1.01 at 1,000 points and runs down to 45 at the 32nd and last pick. Dollars are points over ten, so the first pick is $100. The 24th pick — the middle of the second round, the thing people mean when they say “a mid-second” — is 100 points. Ten dollars. One tenth of the top pick.

Averaged across its sixteen picks, the whole second round comes to 99.7 points a pick: call it ten dollars for any second you cannot place. And added together, all sixteen second-rounders are worth 1,595 points — less than picks one and two on their own, which are 1,860.

The entire second round is worth less than the top two picks.
So why does one cost fifteen dollars?

Because that is what the market charges. The price guide, calibrated to 235 real trades in this league, puts a future second at $15 — and $15 on this chart is 150 points, which is the 19th pick. The third selection of the second round. Not the middle of it.

That gap is the whole lesson. Buying an unplaced future second costs about half again what the pick is likely to become. You are not paying for a player; you are paying for the option on one, and the option is dearer than the thing. Sellers of future seconds in this league are getting a good price, and buyers are paying a premium for the privilege of not knowing yet.

Where the desk went wrong

Two scales were in play and both of them put the 1.01 at $100, which is exactly why it was so easy to mix them up. DynastyProcess prices players on a superflex curve anchored at $100 for the 1.01. The league chart prices picks on a curve anchored at the same place but deliberately steeper and more top-heavy, because that is how managers here have actually traded for six years. Agreeing at the anchor is not the same as agreeing anywhere else, and the further down the board you go the further apart they get.

Quoting a player's DynastyProcess dollars against a pick's league-chart dollars therefore reads fine and is wrong, and it is wrong in a consistent direction: it makes picks look like real money. They are not. Below about pick 24 they are a rounding error with a round number attached.

This is not theoretical. Pricing Kyler Murray off his DynastyProcess dollars produced $38 and the conclusion that there was no market for him. The league's own trade record says a starting quarterback has cost a first seventeen times in thirty-two deals. The chart and the record disagreed by a full round, and the record was right — it is the one built out of what the sixteen people in this league have actually done.

The working rule

A future 1st is $40 and a current mid-1st is about the same. A future 2nd is $15 but ought to be $10. Anything from pick 28 down is a sweetener you throw in, not a price you pay.

And there are only 32 of these in existence against 304 roster places, which is the other reason a second-round pick cannot buy a starter: the draft is not where this league's players come from.

How this was worked out. Every figure is read straight off the two calibrated numbers the league already keeps: the pickValueChart in draft-intel.json (pick 1 = 1000 points, dollars = points over ten) and the priceGuide in the same file, which is calibrated to 235 real SCFL trades across six years.
