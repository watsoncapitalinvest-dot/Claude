Ten Things The Ledger Says

245 two-team trades, priced at the season each one was made and read ten ways, then twice more for each of the sixteen franchises — once for what is going right and once for what is not. Fairness turns out to have nothing to do with winning, the median deal turns out to decide everything, and most of this league is good at exactly half of the job.

  1. 01−0.01

    Being fair tells you nothing about whether you win

    Across the thirteen ranked franchises, the correlation between how often a side’s deals come out level and how many dollars it ends up with is −0.01. Not weak — absent. The Machines are the fairest side in the league at 67 % level and have the best record. Powers of Pain come out level least often of anybody at 38 % and finish third on money. Thirteen points is not a study, but there is no hint of a relationship to chase.

  2. 020.77

    It is a grind, not a heist

    The single best predictor of what a franchise is worth across the whole ledger is its median deal — correlation 0.77. Its best ever trade manages +0.66 and its worst −0.12. Nobody in this league got rich on one afternoon: the medians that separate Powers of Pain at the top from Wookie Leaks at the bottom run from $6.80 to −$6.80 a deal. That is the whole league, $13.60 wide.

  3. 0310/13

    Almost nobody is simply good at trading

    Ten of the thirteen gain on one half of the ledger and lose on the other — up on players and down on picks, or the reverse. Only three move both the same way, and they are the ones at the ends: Pork Chop Express (first), Powers of Pain (third) and Wookie Leaks (thirteenth). The top and the bottom are consistent. Everyone in between is good at exactly one thing, and the table does not tell them which.

  4. 04$91

    The two best traders in the league are mirror images

    Pork Chop Express: $258 on players, $7 on picks. The Machines: −$35 on players, $209 on picks. $265 and $174 — nearly the same destination by opposite roads. One buys the man, the other buys the slot, and neither does the other’s job at all.

  5. 050.26

    Volume buys nothing

    Deals made against dollars made: 0.26. Deals made against how often you come out level: 0.09. Pork Chop Express made 66 and finished $265; Lil Chops made 53 and finished −$29. Hairy Gumbas made four and are up $27. Picking up the phone is not a strategy.

  6. 0678%

    The money is held by three men

    Six of the thirteen ranked franchises are ahead and seven behind, which sounds like a league in balance. It is not. Pork Chop Express, The Machines and Powers of Pain hold 78 % of every dollar on the plus side ($590 of $760). This is not a few sharks and a soft middle. It is three men and then a long flat stretch.

  7. 07$264

    The best pick trader in the league has almost nothing to show for it

    Heavy Hitters are up $264 on picks across 22 deals — the largest gain on either half of the ledger anybody has. Their net is $88, because they hand back $176 on players. The best pick trader in the league is also one of its worst player traders, and the two very nearly cancel.

  8. 0818%

    There are two ways to lose, and the table cannot tell them apart

    Wookie Leaks’ worst single deal is −$53 against a −$289 total — no one trade is more than 18 % of the hole — with a median of −$6.80 across 49 trades, 30 of which went down. That is a leak. Lil Chops took the single biggest loss in the league (−$132, the 2022 deal in the margin, which they signed as The A-Team) and still finish −$29 across 53. One has a habit. The other had a bad Tuesday.

  9. 0979%

    The board is right about four deals in five

    Of the 370 verdicts that can be checked a year on, 292 hold and 78 change — 79 %. And the league-wide split barely moves: fourteen deals that looked level on the day did not stay level, which is seven more wins and the same seven more losses. The wait does not make this league fairer. It moves who is holding the win.

  10. 10$222

    Time is not neutral, and it is not random either

    Lil Chops recover $222 of their day-of position (−$304 to −$82): they sold players who then did not produce. Master Jet-I go the other way, −$53 to −$186. Across all thirteen, the total money on the table falls 9 % over the year, so the typical edge is real and smaller than it looked on the day.

Where the small books count and where they do not

Three franchises have fewer than 10 priced deals: Horse Collars (five), Guido Haters (five) and Hairy Gumbas (four). They sit out the ten findings at the top, where a four-deal sample would drag a league-wide rate a long way for no reason. In the franchise lines they are ranked on everything, like everybody else — with the number of deals beside each name, so a rate off four afternoons is visibly a rate off four afternoons.
