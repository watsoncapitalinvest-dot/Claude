The Wrong Number

The league will vote on a third injured-reserve slot, and all summer both sides argued it by counting how many teams have a full IR. That number does not measure what a slot costs. Four weeks of the season’s own record say what does — and the answer is smaller than one camp claimed and climbing faster than the other could prove in August.

What A Slot Actually Does

Start with the mechanism, because most of the summer went past it. A Sleeper IR slot does not hold a player who would otherwise be sitting in the free-agent pool. It holds a man who is already on your roster. What it frees is an active roster spot — and that spot gets filled off the wire, immediately, because no manager in this league leaves one open.

So the cost of an extra IR spot is not the injured player. It is the healthy replacement his manager signs with the spot that opens up: one player out of the pool, per slot actually used. Which makes the whole question countable instead of arguable, because a manager only uses a new slot if two things are true of him right now — his IR is already full, and he is still carrying an injured man on his active roster. Everybody else costs the pool nothing. They would take the slot and have nobody to put in it.

Both camps counted teams with a full IR. That counts who is inconvenienced, not what a slot costs.

Two things in that table point in opposite directions, and both are true. The pressure the expansion side described is real and it is building — it just did not exist yet when the vote was being argued. In week 1 exactly 1 team in the league was constrained. It did not move at all for the first 2 weeks — still 1 in week 2 — and then went to 5 across weeks 3 and 4. A vote taken in August was voting on the flat part.

Figure Two — At Cap Is Not Constrained

Here is where the counting went wrong. 9 of 16 teams have a full IR, and that is the figure that got quoted all summer. But 4 of those 9 are not holding a single injured player on their active roster. Give them a third slot and nothing happens: they have nobody to move into it and they sign nobody off the wire. Only 5 rosters are in the state that actually spends a slot.

Figure Three — What It Would Cost The Wire

Now the number the vote is actually about. One constrained manager with one stranded player signs one replacement; a manager with four stranded players still only gets one slot, so he still only signs one. Add a fourth slot and he can take a second. That is the entire difference between the two proposals.

The 5 Who Are Not Using What They Have

One more thing the record turns up, and it is awkward for everybody. Beaver Eaters, Smoke Dragons, The Powers of Pain, Killer Klowns, Guido Haters are carrying an injured player on an active roster right now while sitting under the current cap. The slot they are asking about, or voting against, is a slot they already own and have not filled. Whatever the vote decides, it will not do anything for a manager who has not used number two.

What This Settles

A third slot is cheap. 5 players, 0.99 per cent of the wire, 5 teams affected. The objection that expansion would strip the free-agent pool is not supported by the season: the pool has sat flat around 506 for 4 weeks while IR use climbed from 12 slots to 21.

A fourth buys almost nothing. It adds 1 player and 1 team over the third. If three passes, four is not worth a second ballot.
