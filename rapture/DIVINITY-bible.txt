DIVINITY
========


STORY BIBLE
-----------


A feature screenplay. Approx. 128 pages. Written for R, softenable. Upstate New York and the Adirondacks, across four years.

Logline. The Rapture leaves billions dead and the survivors certain they were damned. Four years later a settlement in the Adirondack forest is still keeping a law nobody is left to enforce — and the new world has come to take it.


____________________________________________________________


1 · THE ARGUMENT
================


The question: is goodness worth anything if there is no reward and nobody is watching?

The film puts the strongest possible case for no in the villain's mouth and lets him be right about every fact he cites.

The thesis, precisely. What holds Eden together is not loyalty to each other. It is dedication to the morality Jesus and the Bible taught, kept by people who believe there is no longer any higher power to judge them. They are not a family that survives. They are a community keeping a law nobody is enforcing.

The shape of it: they live under the Law, and the ending is Grace. They keep the commandments perfectly, for four years, with nobody enforcing them. It cannot save them. Then it is given anyway.

No character ever states any of this.


____________________________________________________________


2 · THE WORLD
=============


THE RAPTURE
-----------

- The taken leave bodies. No vanishing, no piles of clothes. Corpses, mass graves, disease, a world that has to be physically dug out of.
- No nameable cause of death. Science reads that as "we haven't found it yet." Faith reads it as "there was nothing to find." Both sides get real evidence, nobody wins the argument, and it gets settled with violence.
- The pattern is messy. Not all children. Not all Christians. Devout people left standing beside atheists who were taken; whole churches emptied and the one next door untouched. Legible enough to feel like judgment, inconsistent enough to deny.
- It happens everywhere at the same instant. Nothing precedes it and nothing can be reported in advance.
- Almost everyone alive concludes the same thing anyway: they were judged, they failed, and being left behind is hell.


WHAT IT ACTUALLY WAS
--------------------

A real judgment. And also a selection. The film never says which applies to whom, and nobody on the ground can tell.

Among the billions left behind, some were left because they were judged — and some were chosen, for an honest search for faith and a fundamental commitment to good. Not victims. Heaven's first wave.

Eden has spent four years believing it was rejected. It was deployed.

Nobody in the film ever suspects this. The audience understands it from one piece of blocking in the finale.


THE MORAL RULE — THE PHYSICS OF THIS WORLD
------------------------------------------

If you are trying to be good, you are good, even if you do a bad thing unknowingly. If you know a thing is evil and you do it anyway, you are doomed.

Intent is everything. It is never stated aloud and no character ever learns it. The audience works it out; the characters never do. That gap is the engine — we know they are safe long before they do, and we watch them despair anyway.

It lands like this:
- Ruthie gave Eden away without knowing, and refused knowingly at the end. Clean, and the clearest case in the film.
- John said a true thing and a man died. He did not intend it. He has spent four years paying a debt that was never owed.
- The church mob splits. Same act, same room, opposite fates: some believed they were doing justice on a monster, some knew it was murder and enjoyed it. The film demonstrates its own moral physics in scene two and the audience cannot see it until much later.
- Ward is the pure case of the second clause. He knew, and opened the door anyway. The rule does not care that he was a victim.
- The people who walk out the gate on the last night are not damned. They are frightened and want to live. John does not hate them and neither does the film.


GOD AND THE ENEMY
-----------------

- God is silent from the first morning until the final battle. The only visible miracles in the film belong to the enemy.
- Demons are real and work by invitation. Ward willingly allowed one into himself. It did not take him; he opened the door. His free will is intact, which keeps him culpable.
- THE SUBVERSION RULE — the film's whole aesthetic. Nothing on the enemy's side ever declares itself. No demon faces, ever. Demons, Ward and the Antichrist work only by subversion, manipulation and influence. Every evil thing in this movie is warm, reasonable and patient. Plant clues that resolve only in retrospect. The only force that ever shows up in the open is the one that arrives at the end.

3 · CHARACTERS
==============


EMMA — 21 AT THE RAPTURE, 25 AT THE SIEGE
-----------------------------------------

Sorority sister, in the back seat when it happens.

Her whole family was taken and she was not — parents and a younger brother of fifteen — and she was the believer in that house. She was the one who went. Her father hadn't set foot in a church since he was a kid. All three are dead in three rooms and she buries them herself in the backyard over two days. That is why, a month later, she knows exactly what she is looking at when she finds a field of graves nobody can account for.

Who she is. Genuinely good at heart. Low confidence. Capable of doing anything that needs doing and not afraid to try. Real empathy, not performed. What stands between her and leadership is other people's judgment of her, and breaking free of it is her arc.

Her function is that she has no function. She delivers a baby, digs a latrine, sits three nights with a dying man talking about nothing, takes the shift nobody wants. Everyone depends on her and nobody can name what she does, because claiming a role means being judged for it.

She can say anything to anyone one-on-one. She cannot say it to a room.

What Eden holds against her. After the seed and the fence, Emma is the girl who brought Ruthie in. Nobody has ever said it. Everyone thinks it. It is completely unfair and it is the exact reason she will not stand up in a meeting.

Her turn: she answers Ward at the wall, in front of everyone, on behalf of everyone, knowing precisely how it looks coming from her.


JOHN — 37
---------

A contractor with one company — the thing he has spent his adult life trying to make into something.

Twice it has come within reach and twice a big loss has knocked it back to nothing. The first was betrayal: someone he believed in took it out from under him. The second was pure chance — a bank folded, a job died, nothing and nobody to blame. After the betrayal he learned the lesson everyone tells you to learn, applied it, and got flattened anyway. One loss had a villain and one had nothing at all, and no theory covers both.

The second time, he had already decided God had helped him and said so out loud to people.

He is three weeks into that when the film starts. Not angry — past angry, because anger takes hope. Polite, hollow, falling apart.

He is at church that morning for an accounting, not for comfort. He came to ask what he did wrong. He gets an answer ninety seconds later, when every faithful person in the room drops dead and he is still standing there breathing.

The church. When the congregation realizes the priest was not taken, John is the one who says out loud that the priest may have been judged. He argues against killing him and fails. He is not wrong and he does not swing, and a man dies anyway.

And neither does Ward. The young man who told the room what was done to him does not swing either. He only watches. He gets his revenge in the first ten minutes and does not even get to do it with his own hands — the room takes it from him while he stands there. That is what he spends four years unable to forgive.

His damage — and it is not muteness. He talks easily. Dry, decent company, will discuss drainage for an hour. What he will not do is assert. He asks instead of answering and puts every decision back to the room, because the last time he stated a conviction in front of a crowd a man died on the floor. Eden is led for four years by a man who has never given an order. People follow him anyway, which frustrates everyone including him.

He buries people. Strangers, in the thousands, through all of act one, while the rest of the world steps over them. He can't say why. Digging is his sacrament and it is something to do with his hands so he doesn't have to be a person.

THE ARC LOCK — Eden is his third build. He built twice for himself and lost both. The third he builds for nothing: no money, no name, no return, and by then no belief that anyone is keeping score. When it is about to come down a third time, exactly the way it always does, the sky opens. The pattern of his entire life breaks, and it breaks on the one thing he didn't build for himself.


EMMA AND JOHN
-------------

Deeply in love. Unspoken. Never resolved.

The engine under everything, and neither ever names it. The actors play it; the script never states it. It lives in behaviour: who gets the last of something, whose opinion changes his mind, the fact that he stops digging when she talks.

Two rules keep it honest:
1. Neither ever names it. The audience is never told what to think because the characters don't know either.
2. Somebody in Eden says it out loud once — one disapproving line about the age of her and the age of him — and it isn't resolved and nobody raises it again. It lands on Emma far harder than on him. That line is what makes the film aware of the gap rather than oblivious to it.

The closest it ever comes: a hand held on the wall when they both think it's over, and one of them starting a sentence and not finishing it.


RUTHIE — 22
-----------

Emma's friend, the other survivor of the crash. Sweet, likable, weak and easily manipulated. Not scheming. A scared kid who bends toward whoever is kind to her. What she actually wants is to be worth something, in a camp where nobody thinks she is useful and has told her so with their eyes all winter.

Why she takes the seed — she is not stealing it, she thinks she is rescuing it. Two trips.

First trip, empty-handed. A two-day walk to the checkpoint in February, because a scavenging party came back talking about a generator and she wants to see a light somebody else turned on. Ward feeds her, listens, asks easy questions — how far did you walk, is anybody sick, are there children, how many of you are there. Then he gives her what she actually wants: absolution. None of it is your fault, nobody's keeping score, you're a good girl.

He asks her for nothing. He only mentions that the government is collecting viable seed stock — a real programme, catalogued, shipped, planted, feeding people who are starving right now.

Every word of it is true. He never lies to her once.

The fortnight. Forty-odd pounds of seed sitting in a hole doing nothing. Delia has said in front of everyone that they'll eat it or die with it. It is going down somebody's throat in February. And there is a place two days south that will put it in the ground. She works out that she is the only person up there who can save it. It is very nearly right. That is the awful part.

Second trip. She takes the sealed tins — what one person can carry for two days — and leaves the open sacks. She hands them over and a man writes it in a ledger and gives her a receipt, a slip of paper with a stamp on it, which is exactly what a real programme gives you. She walks back in through the gate holding it out in front of her, because she is expecting to be thanked.

What she gave besides the seed is useless as a location: two days' walk northwest, a river she can't name, a ridge that looks like a ridge. In six million acres that is a rumour.

The eight days. She is caught and then nothing happens at all. They have a law that tells them exactly what to do and not one of them can bring themselves to do it, and they can't set it aside either. So nobody says anything, and for eight days Ruthie hauls water and turns compost in a settlement where not one person will meet her eye. She would have taken a sentence. A sentence would have been a mercy.

She sentences herself. She packed on the fourth day, because she can read the board as well as anyone and she knows what it says about her. "Nobody's putting you out, Ruthie." — "You don't have to."

Her death — she works out the trap by herself. She walks two days back to the fence because it is the only other place she knows and because he was kind to her. Ward asks her to walk them in. She says no. And he lets her go — pleasantly, no threats — because he never needed her cooperation. He needed her to go home, with two men half a mile behind her.

The girl everyone in this story has called weak sits in that tent with the soup going cold and understands the whole shape of it, alone.

So she goes east. Away from the ridge, away from the river, into country she doesn't know, for a day and a half, until the men following her understand she is never going home. They kill her out there, a long way from anything.

Nobody in Eden hears a thing. Emma will not stop looking. On the fifth day they find her twenty-odd miles east, in the wrong drainage, pointed the wrong way. It looks like she got lost — small, confused, stupid — and that is what every person in the settlement quietly believes for the next four years. They carry her home. Four days. Emma does not put her end down.

Her refusal is why Eden exists at all. The last thing she did on earth was ruin Ward's map, and it cost him four years. Not one of them knows.


SIMON WARD
----------

The parishioner the priest molested. Unnamed and forgettable in the church scene — a shaking young man in the third row telling the room what was done to him when he was nine. His testimony is what pushes the congregation over the edge.


THE SHOT THE WHOLE CHARACTER DEPENDS ON

After the killing, everyone has gone. John comes back at dusk and digs.

Ward is still there — in a pew in the dark, or at the treeline — watching him bury the priest. John never sees him. The audience does. He gets up and walks out the other door.

Five seconds, no dialogue. Without it the audience never connects the parishioner to the man at the wall, and the character does not work.


WHAT HE HAS BEEN DOING FOR FOUR YEARS

He wanted to kill that priest himself. He never lays a hand on him. He stands in the third row and watches forty people do it for him, and it fixes nothing. He told a room the worst thing that ever happened to him and the room took it out of his hands while he stood there.

So he has been hunting that congregation. One at a time. For four years. Not because they were wrong. Because they stole it from him.

The map on his wall has rivers crossed off. Beside it is a list of names, most of them struck through.


WHICH MEANS THE SIEGE WAS NEVER ABOUT THE SETTLEMENT

Forty-one people are incidental. He came for the last two men from that church: John and Ray.

He has known since the fence — Ruthie told him there was a man up there who buries people, and he had once watched a man bury somebody.

And when Eden turns on Ray in act three, they are two days from handing Ward, personally, one of the two things he came four years to collect. Nobody inside the walls has any idea.


THE TWO WANTS

The demon in him wants Eden — despair, conversion, a soldier turned. That is what makes him useful to the President and why his obsession is tolerated: it happens to point at the right place.

The man in him wants John. He wants him to walk out of that gate first, and then to do the thing forty people did without him.

He gets neither.


THE BRUTALITY — SHOWN ONCE

A house door opens on a man who has been hiding eighteen months. Ward is polite. He says something quiet and reassuring and the man's shoulders come down. Then somebody shoots him, and Ward is already walking back down the path. He never raises his voice. His face does not change.

Same voice for the kindness and the killing. He is not two people. The warmth is not a mask over cruelty — it is the same instrument pointed differently, and it costs him nothing to swing it.

    RULE: Ward never shouts, never threatens, never gloats and never visibly enjoys anything. Not once in the film. The moment he raises his voice he becomes an ordinary villain.


HE IS A DESERTER

Ward was chosen too — in that church, honestly searching, a wounded man looking for God on the worst morning in history. Same wave, same criteria. And he defected. He is not a sinner; he is a deserter, the one man in the film who knew exactly what he was turning down.

Surname note: a ward is a child in someone's care — what he was. It is also the wall of a fortress — the thing he comes to break.


THE PRESIDENT — THE ANTICHRIST
------------------------------

He is the sitting President of the United States, and he was not taken.

President for two years, liked for twenty before that. The decent one, the competent one, the one everyone always said ought to be running things back when it was a joke. Nothing about him was ever real — twenty years of public service built patiently for the morning the chair came open. The audience does not learn this until the end. He is outside the moral rule; it does not apply to him.

He does not rise. He does not have to. Everyone around him simply goes — two-thirds of Congress, four of the Court, the generals who could have said no and the lawyers whose whole job was telling him what he could not do. The Rapture does not make him king. It removes every restraint on a man who already was one.

And the world notices he is still breathing. Every pulpit and kitchen table asks it that first month. He answers it himself, on television, and it is the most dangerous sentence of the year:

    "If there had been a judgment — would I be standing here?"

His own non-selection becomes the proof of his doctrine.

His early acts are genuinely good. He restores the grid, moves food, clears the dead. People are not fools for loving him; they are right, for a while. The turn is slow and every step is reasonable, and by the time anyone can name what he has become he is the only thing between the world and starvation.

His pitch is absolution. There was no judgment. You are not guilty. No one is keeping score. Mercy to a planet drowning in guilt — and permission. He empties the word sin and hands it back, and they love him for it, because he is the only man who told them they were innocent. To forty-one people in a forest it is an order to stand down.


THE SIGN — COUNTERFEIT OF SPECIFIC SCRIPTURE, NEVER INVENTED

THE MORTAL WOUND — Revelation 13:3. And I saw one of his heads as it were wounded to death; and his deadly wound was healed: and all the world wondered after the beast.

In the second year a man in a crowd in Cleveland shoots him twice through the chest on live television. Six hundred million people watch him go down, watch the blood come through the shirt, watch a doctor step back, watch the sheet go over. For one hour and forty minutes the world is told the President is dead.

Then he sits up. He walks out under his own power with the sheet over his arm and blood dried down his front, and forgives the man who did it by name.

There is no argument on earth after that. Nobody needs a doctrine any more. They have seen it.

    The rhyme this buys: the enemy counterfeits resurrection in act two — and in act three the real thing happens to nineteen farmers in the mud.

(Available if wanted: fire out of heaven in the sight of men, Rev 13:13. Generic faith-healing is invented and reads as invention — do not use it.)


THE EMPTY ROOM

One scene, mid-film, alone. No aides, no camera. The warmth switches off, and not into menace — into nothing. No cruelty, no gloating, no face. He sits and idles like a machine. A door opens and the warmth comes straight back on, mid-sentence, perfect.

Reads as "tired man" the first time and as something else entirely the second. Helen March is the one who walks in on it.


HELEN MARCH — 40S, THE PRESIDENT'S CHIEF OF STAFF
-------------------------------------------------

Brilliant, decent, loved him, gave him everything. She works out what he is around the midpoint and stays. She is the moral rule's second clause dramatized in someone the audience likes: Ward is damned by consent, Helen by comfort. She says nothing, then or ever. At the end she watches the sky open on a monitor and understands exactly what it means.


THE BENCH
---------


DELIA VANCE, 50s — the nurse. ER charge nurse before. Competent, unsentimental, Eden's de facto second. She and John disagree constantly and trust each other completely. She triaged for nine days after the Rapture and had to decide who got treated; she has been sorting people ever since and knows it. She argued hardest to exile Ruthie, on arithmetic, not cruelty. The case for judgment, made by someone who is usually right.

HAZEL, 70s — the one who still prays. Widow; her husband was taken and she wasn't. Prays out loud, daily, in front of everyone, unembarrassed. She wrote the board. Once she hears about Ward she starts praying for him by name and everyone hates it. She dies before the sky opens — the only person who would have understood it, missing it by an hour. Then she is the first one to stand back up, and she is laughing.

RAY WHELAN, 60s — the man from the church. Was in that room. Came out with John. Talks all day while he works, about weather and hockey and a dog he had in 1988 — and has never once mentioned that morning. Eden's carpenter. In act three he is accused of being the traitor and will not defend himself, because defending himself means saying where he was. That is what finally makes John commit to something out loud.

ADAM, 19 — the one who wants the gate locked. Arrived at 14. The loudest person in Eden: argues, complains, jokes, needles John from dawn to dark. His family did not die in the Rapture. They died four months after it, at the hands of people, and he watched. That is why he wants the wall held, and he can never say so, because the first law means nobody asks and he was raised on not telling. So he argues badly, sounds paranoid, gets dismissed — and he is right the entire time. On the last night he is the one who holds the gate open for the children.

EVELYN — "Evie" — 17, the gardener. Arrived at 13 in a dead man's overcoat three sizes too big that she would not take off indoors or out, and told Delia on her second morning that the woodpile was stacked wrong and would be rotten by February. It was, and it was. No silent period at all — she has been telling people what they're doing wrong since the week she arrived. What she does not do is argue about people; in five years nobody has heard her take a side about a person in front of a room, which is why four words from her move it.

She plants the seed over Ruthie's grave at thirteen. Asked what she thinks she's doing, she says it's late in the year for it.

Emma took her in — and Emma's brother was fifteen and a nightmare and was taken, and this girl is thirteen and impossible and was left. Eight years between them: sisters, not mother and daughter. She is Emma's mirror. Emma cannot speak because she cannot bear being judged; Evie speaks because it does not occur to her to care. That is what Emma is looking at when she rises.

TOBY, 6 — the child. Doesn't remember the world; thinks the Rapture is a story adults tell. Follows John around while he digs. Goes out through the gate on the last night.

PAUL DIETZ, 40s — the one who walks out. Ordinary, decent, a father. Takes the terms for his kids' sake. The film must not condemn him — under the rule he isn't damned, he's frightened, and John lets him go without a word.

GREER — the man Ward sends in. Quiet, grateful, useful. We watch Ward send him, so this is never a whodunnit. His job isn't sabotage — Ward already knows roughly where Eden is. His job is to find the weak ones and plant the idea that somebody inside betrayed them. He is vetted, argued over for two days, and admitted. Adam votes against him and can't say why and loses. Careful people getting it wrong is far worse than a loophole.


ADAM AND EVELYN — THE FINAL COUPLE
----------------------------------

Their romance is plain and unembarrassed — the deliberate contrast with Emma and John, who cannot say it in four years while these two never had to.

Their entire relationship is one recurring image: Evie working the outer field, Adam standing at the edge of it with a rifle because he doesn't want her out there alone. Nobody ever comments on it.

How he tells her about his family: once, in the dark, to one person, and we never see what he describes. She is the only living soul who knows. The law forbade asking; it never forbade telling. Volunteering your history is the one intimacy this world has. That is their love scene and there is no nudity in it and no dialogue we hear.

    NAMING RULE — never underline it. She is Evie for the whole film. Nobody ever says "Eve." The final scene has no dialogue at all. The audience assembles Adam, Evelyn, a garden, an apple tree and a snake by themselves, and the moment it is said aloud you have taken that away from them.

4 · EDEN
========


FOUNDING — TWO STAGES
---------------------

The camp. They stop at a treeline and fail to leave. It is never decided; nobody votes to found anything. They survive, they hoard, they do not plant, because nobody believes in next year. By the time it has walls John has been running it for a year without once agreeing to.

Eden. Ruthie gives away the seed and dies. They plant what is left of it — the damp, partial, half-dead remainder she couldn't carry — over her grave. The place takes the name the day they bury her. Things grow there out of season, too green, too fast. Nobody says it out loud, nobody explains it, and blessing versus wishful thinking is never settled.

    The arithmetic does not start failing in year four. It starts failing at Ruthie's funeral, with bad seed, and nobody notices for three years.


THE LAW
-------

Eden is governed by the Ten Commandments. Not an invented charter. The actual Law, kept by people who believe the Lawgiver has gone.

THE BOARD. Ten lines burned into a plank on the wall of the meeting hall. Hazel wrote them from memory in the first winter, because there was not a Bible in that camp and there wouldn't be one for two more years.

Two years later a family comes in off the Northway with a Bible in a bread bag, and somebody works through the board against it. One of the ten is wrong.

They leave it. It has been true for four years. (The law they keep is imperfect, human and slightly mis-remembered, and they keep it anyway. Do not explain this. Put it on the wall and let one character notice.)

THE TWO HOUSE RULES. Underneath the ten, two more lines, and Hazel tells everyone — then and every time after — that these two are hers and not God's:

    Nobody is asked what they did before the treeline.
    Nobody is kept here who wants to go.

The Law is harsh. These two are Eden's own mercy, and everyone knows the difference.


HOW THE COMMANDMENTS DRIVE THE STORY
------------------------------------

FIRST — no other gods. This is the third act. A man on television died on camera and got back up and the world worships him. Ward stands outside the wall asking nineteen people to accept a god who demonstrably performs miracles over one who has not said a word in four years. It is not an abstraction to them. It is the thing being demanded at gunpoint.

FOURTH — the Sabbath. One day in seven, nothing gets done. They keep it during the siege — a settlement under occupation putting its tools down because a law nobody is enforcing says to. There is a ten-minute argument about it that Hazel wins by not saying anything at all. Ward stands in the wet grass and watches, and it is the only time in the film his face changes.

FIFTH — honour thy father and mother. With everyone's parents dead or taken this becomes the keeping of the dead: names on pickets, graves squared and mounded, and Ruthie's name staying on the garden.

SIXTH — thou shalt not kill. The founding sin, committed in a church before Eden existed. And what the siege forces them back into.

EIGHTH — thou shalt not steal. Ruthie's commandment. The Law is not merciful about theft: restitution, doubled (Exodus 22), which she can never make. Hazel's two lines are no help and Hazel says so — nobody asks what you did before the treeline, and she didn't do it before the treeline, she did it here.

    THE BEST STRUCTURAL THING IN THE FILM. What John stands up to argue is not on that board and not in Hazel's two lines. There is no clause in the twelve covering a girl who owes a debt she can never repay and ought to be forgiven it anyway.

    John — who has no faith, hasn't prayed in four years, and would tell you flatly nobody is listening — stands up in that room and argues for grace. He does not know that is what it is.

    It is the exact thing the sky gives all of them at the end. The one argument he ever makes is the one the universe turns out to agree with.

NINTH — bear no false witness. What the traitor-hunt destroys. Act three is Eden breaking its own ninth commandment in a panic.

TENTH — covet. The enemy's entire offer. Come out, have what they have. Covetousness dressed as mercy, which is what it always is.


THE WALL
--------

Real, guarded, and barred every night of its life. That is not a moral failing — it is the reason there are children alive behind it. Eden is not an open camp and was never meant to be one.

The fourth house rule is about leaving, not entering. What Eden refuses to do is imprison its own people.

Admission is a decision, not an obligation. Argued out every time, on whether they can be fed and what a person can do. It sits alongside the first house rule without contradiction:

    You are not asked what you did. You are asked what you'll do.

John built it over four summers. It kept out weather and bears and, twice, men. It is the best thing he ever made and it worked every day of its life — until the morning something arrived that it was never going to stop. That is not a failure. That is what walls are.


FOOD LOGIC
----------

Arithmetic, not atmosphere. These are numbers the film should be able to defend.

The seed. Eaten, ~40 lbs is about a week of food for nineteen people — nothing. Planted, it is three to four acres: a year of food for forty. Delia says it out loud once. Ruthie did not take their supper. She took the only future they had, and everyone understood that within a minute. That is why the reaction is so cold and why nobody actually starved.

Delia's ledger, year four. 41 people crossing an Adirondack winter need roughly 15,000 lbs of stored food (≈2 lbs/person/day × 180 days), and that is exactly what Eden has. 31 refugees takes the requirement to ~26,000 lbs.

Her objection is not we'll starve. It is sharper, and she is right:
    At full ration we're empty on the fourteenth of February. At two-thirds we're empty the third week of March and every one of us is too weak to plant in April.

A starved community loses the following year too.

THE ANOMALY — measurable and deniable. Delia weighs the potato store on the first of every month, as always.
- February should read 4,100 lbs. It reads 5,900.
- She re-derives the October intake: same answer. She watches the pot to see if people are eating less: they aren't. She looks for a hidden cache: there isn't one.
- By April they should be at zero. They are at 2,200 and the first greens are in.

Every deniable explanation is genuinely available — the second field out-yielded what she logged, the October count was rushed, people forage more than they report, the refugees brought more than she recorded. Each is possible. None is provable. All of them together still don't close the gap.

Nobody sees a miracle. There is a woman with a pencil who cannot make her own ledger balance and will not say the word.


PART THREE'S CONFLICT — THE WIDOW OF ZAREPHATH
----------------------------------------------

1 Kings 17. The widow is down to her last handful of meal, about to make a final meal for herself and her son and then die, and Elijah asks her to feed him first. She does. And the barrel of meal did not waste, neither did the cruse of oil fail.

1. Refugees arrive — more mouths than Eden can carry through a winter.
2. They take them anyway. The argument runs an hour: Delia's ledger says March; Adam wants the gate shut and argues it loudly and badly; Emma has the answer and cannot make herself stand up. Then Evie — who never takes a side about a person in front of a room — says four words: we'll grow more. Emma watches the girl she raised do the thing she cannot do.
3. The stores don't empty.

The argument that follows is not "do we have enough." It is "are we allowed to count on this." Do you plan around a thing you cannot explain — is that trust, or is it stupidity? Faith argued as a purely practical question, with nobody ever saying the word. Hazel names Zarephath. Nobody believes her. Delia goes back to the ledger.


THE LAMP
--------

One lamp in the meeting hall they cannot afford to keep burning, and it does not go out. Remarked on once, never again. It is the last light still on when the sky opens.

5 · STRUCTURE
=============


Total span: four years.


PART ONE — THE FIRST DAYS (PP. 1–38)

The car. The church. Ward staying behind to watch John dig. Two threads cut against each other with no geography given — Emma and Ruthie walking, John digging, the audience holding both halves for twenty pages and never joining them. The site. The trench, where Ray joins him. The nine. The three days that cost a boy his life because John wouldn't call it. The prayer that comes out as an account. Then Emma comes over a rise and there is a man in a hole, and she picks up the second shovel.

Nobody has to explain John. She has been reading him for weeks in the only language he has.


PART TWO — THE FIRST WINTER (PP. 38–62)

Not Eden yet — a camp. The seed nobody will plant. Hazel and the board. Ruthie's two walks to the fence and the receipt. The eight days. The meeting. Her self-exile, her refusal, the wrong direction, and the five days it takes to find her. The burial. Evie and the half-dead seed. The place takes its name.


THE MONTAGE (PP. 62–70)

Four years, scored, carried on the President's own voice. Consolidation, the grid, food, the doctrine. The mortal wound. Helen at his shoulder. The doorway killing. The clipboard becoming a uniform becoming Deputy Administrator Ward, the pins in the map and the list of names beside it.

This is also where the ugly logistics — bodies, burning, disease — get disposed of in six seconds each instead of forty minutes.


PART THREE — EDEN (PP. 70–95) — INTERLEAVED

Green, working, children who have never known anything else. Emma at twenty-five. The doorway, and the line somebody says about her and John. Greer arrives, is argued over, is admitted. The refugees, the ledger, Zarephath, the lamp. The empty room. Greer's idea taking root. Adam's boot print. Ward's last pin, and two names not struck through.

Cut continuously to the outside. Play dramatic irony, not mystery: we SEE Ward send Greer in. The question is never who is the mole — it is when do they find out.


PART FOUR — THE SIEGE (PP. 95–128)

The occupation. The terms. The hole. The Sabbath. The traitor-hunt and the collapse of the first law. Ray. John's turn. Ward's weapon and what he doesn't understand about it. The last night and the gate. Emma's answer. The order. The wall. The sky.


EPILOGUE — THE GARDEN (PP. 126–128)


WHAT THE ENSEMBLE STRUCTURE COSTS

- Budget. Stadiums, checkpoints, a government reorganizing a world.
- Cast. Eden needs six or seven people worth grieving for; the outside needs its own faces.
- The angels must top the President. If we watch a man die on live television and get up, the sky opening has to be an order of magnitude beyond it.

6 · THE SIEGE
=============


THE WALL DOES NOT STOP HIM
--------------------------

Ward has four hundred men, vehicles and heavy weapons. He could take that wall in twenty minutes and every person inside knows it.

They are not under siege. They are alive at his discretion. What protects them is not the wall — it is what he wants.

THE HOLE. He proves it once, early, and then never uses it again. He puts one round through a section of wall and takes it down. And then nobody comes through the hole.

He leaves it open. They cannot repair it — Ray goes out with timber and a round goes into the ground eight feet from him, and that is the whole message — so two people stand at a gap in their own wall every night, in the cold, watching nothing come through it, while a field kitchen cooks two hundred yards away.

That is the entire siege in one image: your wall is nothing. I'm not coming. Come out.

The question was never whether the wall holds. It is whether the people do. Every hour they are still in there is an hour they chose it again, with a hole in the wall and a hot meal in smelling distance.


WHAT WARD WANTS
---------------

Not the land. Their despair — and, privately, two specific men. Killing them all is his failure state: every hour he spends being reasonable is an hour he is trying to win.

He offers them, one at a time, what he once offered a scared girl at a fence: you were never judged, you are not guilty, no one is keeping score, come out and eat. Warm, patient, no threats.


HIS CLOCK
---------

He does not have all the time in the world. He is feeding four hundred men in a forest off a road nine miles back, in the fall. He has until the snow shuts that road — five weeks, maybe six.

He says he'll wait forever. Delia does his arithmetic and works out that he's lying. So Eden's plan is to outlast him, and it very nearly works.

He attacks because his own clock runs out. He cannot turn them before the snow and cannot withdraw having failed. A tantrum with a deadline — and Eden came within days of winning on arithmetic alone.


THE COLLAPSE FROM INSIDE
------------------------

Somebody must have told them. Who came here and never said what they did? And Eden — founded on never asking — starts asking. The first law dies before the army moves. The walls come down from the inside, and the thing that breaks them is judgment: the exact thing they built the place to escape.


THE LAST NIGHT
--------------

The gate has been barred every night for four years. John has it unbarred and swung wide — not because a rule compels it, but because nobody in Eden is kept here who wants to go, and tonight that means something.

The children go first. Adam — who has wanted that gate shut since he was fourteen — holds it open while they go through, then refuses to leave himself. Paul Dietz goes for his kids and John doesn't say a word to him and doesn't need to. Emma tells Evie to go and Evie refuses her, the only time in four years she ever has, and it is the last conversation they have.

Nineteen people are standing there at dawn, and every one of them chose it twice with the door wide open.


EMMA'S ANSWER
-------------

Ward makes his last offer at first light and it is the kindest thing he has ever said. And Emma answers it — in front of everyone, on behalf of everyone, knowing exactly how it looks coming from her.

She answers with the First Commandment. She has seen the tape; everyone has. She won't claim to know what that man on television is, and she won't pretend she's been given one reason in four years to think anything is listening up here. She says the first one on their board is first for a reason, and it hasn't got a clause about who else might be doing miracles. They worked out a long time ago there's no reward in this. They stayed anyway.


WHAT ACTUALLY HOLDS THE WALL
----------------------------

Ward is trying to make nineteen people lose their faith and they haven't got any. Emma's family was taken and she wasn't. John prayed and got the end of the world. Nobody in Eden believes anyone is coming.

What holds is a law they were taught and never stopped keeping. So the sky opens over people who never believed a word of it — grace, not merit, in one image.

7 · THE FINALE
==============


THE REVEAL — DONE ENTIRELY IN BLOCKING
--------------------------------------

When the sky opens, the angels form up on the line beside them. Not in front. Not instead of. Beside. Nobody says a word about it.

The angels do not come to save them. They come to join them.


THE FALLEN GET UP
-----------------

Not "humans become angels" — revealed, not promoted. A defender takes a round, goes down in the mud, and stands. She is fading at the edges now like everything else on that field, and she picks her rifle back up.

The defenders are outnumbered two hundred to one and the enemy's only weapon stops working. They did not earn it by dying. It is what they always were.

- Hazel is first, out of the mud, glasses gone, laughing — the way you laugh when somebody finally tells you the joke you've been waiting sixty years for.
- Ruthie has been there four years, waiting. Emma sees her. She is not sorry and not ashamed and not apologizing to anybody, for the first time in her life.
- John buried thousands. At the end, nothing stays buried.


THE LOOK
--------

Angels: a Renaissance painting come to life. Gold light with no source, impossible anatomy, six wings where four would do, faces that will not resolve no matter how long you look.

Demons: the same materials with the paint gone wrong — Bosch and Goya.

Both fade at the edges, as if between dimensions. Both sides are visitors. Only Eden is here — until the humans start to fade too.


THE SORTING
-----------

Heaven arrives and the men who are only men break and run — and are allowed to. Nobody stops them, nobody chases them. What stays standing does not run, and that is where the no-faces rule finally breaks. Once. Never again.


MICHAEL, THE PRESIDENT, AND WARD
--------------------------------

Michael takes the beast. Not a human — keep the scales honest. The humans fight the army; an archangel takes the thing that was never a man.

As it goes it takes Ward with it, and the last thing he learns is that he was never a partner. He was equipment.

And he reaches — for John. He has been coming toward that man for four years, across six million acres and a list of forty names, and he is the last of them, and he is being taken away from him like everything else was.

And John — who could not stop one killing in a church four years ago — goes for him. And it is not enough. Ward knew, and chose, and the rule does not bend for anybody, not even for a nine-year-old boy in the third row of a church that failed him.

John tried this time. He was always clean. That closes the church scene.


THE HANDOFF
-----------

Emma goes down and gets up, fading. She has a few seconds and she spends every one of them looking at Evie. No dialogue. The whole inheritance in one look.

John is beside her, not carrying anything, for the first time in the film.

Then they go. All of them. The field empties. The lamp is still burning.

8 · THE ENDING
==============


The risen go with the host when the field is finished. The only two left standing in the flesh are the two who never fell: Adam and Evie.

Emma and John are left behind a second time — and this time it is the assignment, not a rejection. The whole film is people who believed being left was a verdict; the last five minutes say without a word that being left is the job.

Final image: two young people, in ivy, in a garden that four dead people built for them. Neither of them earned it. That is the point.

Evie is planting saplings. Fruit trees, which won't bear for a decade. You do not plant an orchard for yourself, and an audience knows that without being told. Under an apple tree, putting in more apple trees. That is the future tense, and it is the last thing the camera sees before it pulls back.

And something moves in the grass behind her.

    How to play the snake. Played wrong it is nihilism — it was all for nothing, here we go again. Played right it is the opposite: the serpent's return means choice is back. Goodness only means something where refusal is possible; a world with no serpent is a world where nobody chooses anything. The last shot is free will handed back. Same image — the whole film decides which way it reads.

9 · CRAFT RULES
===============


TONE
----

Cold and sparse, building to epic. The Road and Children of Men for two hours — very little said out loud, the camera doing the work, long quiet stretches — then a final twenty minutes at full scale. The restraint is what buys the ending.

With faith-forward moments sprinkled in. The technique: put them in people's mouths, never in the film's voice. Hazel prays out loud and says plain, warm, true things; the film stays cold around her and lets other characters be visibly embarrassed by it. A faith audience gets what it came for; everyone else reads it as characterization. Same lines, two audiences, no compromise.

And it pays off: the one person who said all of it out loud for four years has been dead an hour when the sky opens, and the sparse film hands her the biggest image in it.


RATING
------

Written for R, softenable later. Cutting violence down in a later pass is easy; adding it into a script written soft never works. It matters most in two places: the church killing is slow, clumsy and awful rather than a stylish beat — real people are terrible at this and it takes far too long — and Ruthie's death is as ugly as it should be.


SETTING
-------

The Adirondacks. Six million acres — larger than several states — genuinely trackless, with real −30° winters and a landscape already full of what Eden gets built from: fire towers, ranger cabins, abandoned Great Camps, logging roads that stopped existing decades ago. It makes Ward's four-year search credible. And it sits two hours from Albany and New York, so the regime is close by for the entire film.

The trip is to Atlantic City, not Vegas. Four girls from an upstate school on the Thruway heading south, "Highway to Hell" up loud. Same joke, and it buys a real journey in Part One: the walk north is where Emma passes John's graves. Emma's family is upstate; John's church is Albany or Schenectady; both threads move up the same corridor without the audience ever being told.

Kayla's reason: her mother is in a hospital in Philadelphia and she agreed to drive the girls to Atlantic City if they covered the gas, because it was the only way she could afford to go. The first small kindness in the film, and it is dead in four minutes.


CASTING RULE — NOBODY IS DEFINED BY SILENCE
-------------------------------------------

Everybody in this film talks. Nobody is mute, nobody broods, nobody communicates in meaningful glances. Each person has one specific subject they will not discuss and is perfectly voluble about everything else.

- JOHN talks constantly. What he won't do is commit.
- EMMA is the best talker in the settlement one-on-one. What she cannot do is speak in a room.
- EVIE will talk your ear off about frost dates and drainage. What she doesn't do is argue about people.
- ADAM is the loudest person in Eden. The only subject he won't touch is his family.
- RAY talks all day about weather and hockey and a dog he had in 1988. One morning in his life is off the table.


OTHER STANDING RULES
--------------------

- No demon faces until the last twenty minutes, and then once.
- Ward never raises his voice. Not once.
- Nobody ever says "Eve."
- No song lyrics printed — "Highway to Hell" is a six-figure licence the rights-holders can refuse. Write it in; keep a fallback in the pocket.


PRODUCTION NOTES
----------------

- The final image is a rating conversation. High and wide, pulling away, backlit, in woven and leaf-tied coverings, they read as figures rather than bodies — an achievable PG-13. Decide deliberately, not in the edit.
- The age gap will be read (John 37, Emma 21). The film does the work — somebody says it out loud in act two and it isn't resolved — so it is defensible. Know it is the frame people discuss.
- How wide the final pull-back goes: tight on two people and it is a myth; wide enough to show a green world and it is a restoration; wider still and you must answer what happened to the billions who fled.
