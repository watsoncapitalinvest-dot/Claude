Divinity

The Rapture kills billions in a single morning and leaves the survivors certain they have been damned. Four years later, a settlement in the Adirondacks is still keeping a moral law nobody is left to enforce — and the new world has come to take it.

Format
Feature · ~128 pp
Rating
R · softenable
Genre

Setting
Upstate NY, 4 yrs

The pitch

Everyone who was taken left a body behind. That is the first thing that goes wrong.

There is no wound and no cause of death anybody can name, so the world gets to argue about what happened — and it does, violently, for years. Faith says there was nothing to find. Science says we haven't found it yet. And the pattern of who went is messy: devout people left standing beside atheists who vanished, whole churches emptied and the one next door untouched. Legible enough to feel like a judgment. Inconsistent enough to deny.

Almost everyone left alive reaches the same conclusion anyway. They were judged. They failed. Being left behind is hell, and they are already in it.

Some people decide that if the score no longer counts, nothing is forbidden. And some decide to be good anyway — for no reward, with nobody watching, in a world where the only visible miracles belong to the other side.

Forty-one of those people build a settlement in six million acres of Adirondack forest, on four rules an old woman took straight out of scripture and never pretended she'd written: nobody is asked what they did. Nobody lies about what they do here. What you break, you answer for. The gate is never locked.

Not one of them believes there is anyone left to answer to. They keep the rules regardless — because it is the only decent thing anybody ever offered them, and because it did not stop being true when the one who said it left.

What the enemy wants

The President of the United States was not taken. He does not seize power; everyone around him simply goes — two-thirds of Congress, four of the Court, every general who could have said no. The Rapture doesn't make him king. It removes every restraint on a man who already was one.

And the world notices he's still breathing. He answers the question himself, on television, and it's the most dangerous sentence of the year:

If there had been a judgment — would I be standing here?

Year one

He turns the grid back on. He moves food. He is genuinely good at it, and people are not fools for loving him. Then a man shoots him twice through the chest on live television, and six hundred million people watch the sheet go over — and one hour and forty minutes later he sits up and walks out and forgives the man who did it.

After that, nobody needs a doctrine. But he gives them one anyway: there was no judgment, you are not guilty, nobody is keeping score. To a planet drowning in guilt that is mercy. It is also permission. And to the handful of people still holding a line in a forest, it is an order to stand down.

Who this is about

John, 37 — a contractor

He has one company and it has come within reach twice, and twice a big loss has put him back at the bottom. The second time, he had already decided God helped him get there. He walks into a church on the worst morning in history not for comfort but for an accounting — and ninety seconds later every faithful person in the room is dead on the floor and he is still standing there breathing.

Then he says a true thing out loud about the priest, and the congregation kills the man. John is right, John doesn't swing, and a man dies anyway. He spends the next four years unable to commit to anything. Eden is led for four years by a man who has never once given an order.

Emma, 21 — in the back seat

Her whole family was taken and she was not, and she was the only one in that house who ever went to church. She buries all three of them herself, which is how she recognizes a stranger digging graves for people he never met. She can say anything to anybody one-on-one and cannot say a word in a room, because Eden quietly holds one thing against her and has never once said it out loud.

Ruthie, 22 — the girl in the crash

Sweet, easily led, and desperate to be worth something in a camp that thinks she isn't. She gives away the settlement's seed to a kind man at a checkpoint because he tells her it will be planted and fed to starving people — which is true. Everything that man ever tells her is true. He never needs to lie once.

Simon Ward — the man with the clipboard

The parishioner the priest molested. Unnamed in that church, and his testimony is what turns the room. He got his revenge in the first ten minutes and it fixed nothing. He is the kindest man in this film and the worst thing in it: groomed by a gentle man, he learned exactly how it is done, and everything he does to that girl at the fence was done to him first.

The shape of it

I

The first days

Four girls on the Thruway with "Highway to Hell" up loud, and two of them stop singing. A church where a congregation works out that the priest wasn't taken. A man hanging a door in a building nobody will ever live in, because his hands need something to do. Two people digging graves ninety miles apart, cut against each other with no map given — until one of them comes over a rise and there's a man in a hole, and she picks up the second shovel.

II

The first winter, and four years

A camp that never decides to become a settlement. Forty pounds of seed nobody will plant, because nobody believes in next year. Ruthie's two walks to the fence, and the receipt she carries home expecting to be thanked. Eight days of being frozen out by a place that is forbidden from condemning her. And then the one hour of her life when she sees the trap clearly and walks the wrong way into six million acres to ruin a man's map, and dies out there, and is found five days later pointed away from home — which everyone reads as a girl who got lost.

They plant what she left over her grave. It comes up. The place gets its name.

III

The siege

Four hundred men come out of the trees and do not attack. Ward stands at the wall, unarmed, and offers absolution to forty-one people, one at a time, and says he'll wait as long as it takes. He's lying: he has until the snow shuts the road nine miles back. Inside, the settlement's own first law tears itself apart hunting for whoever gave them away — and the last thing Eden does before the end is decide whether to leave a dead girl's name on the garden.

Then John opens the gate, because the fourth rule is the fourth rule, and the children go out, and we watch who else walks.

The ending — spoilers

They were not left behind

Ward isn't there for the land. He wants their despair — which is why killing them is his failure state, and why he's trying to break a faith that none of them have. Nobody on that wall believes anyone is coming. What holds is a law they were taught and never stopped keeping.

His clock runs out. He gives the order. And he has already lost.

The sky opens, and they come down like a Renaissance ceiling tearing loose — gold light with no source, too many wings, faces that will not resolve — and they take the line beside the defenders. Not in front. Beside.

Because the Rapture was a judgment and a selection, and nobody on the ground could ever tell which was which. These people were chosen — for an honest search and a stubborn decency — and they have spent four years believing they were rejected. They weren't left. They were deployed.

The dead stand back up and pick their rifles up with them, and the enemy's only weapon stops working. An archangel takes the President apart, and it takes Ward with it, and the last thing he learns is that he was never a partner — he was equipment.

Then all of them go, everyone who fell, and the field empties, and the lamp is still burning.

Two young people who never fell are left standing in a garden that four dead people built for them. Neither of them earned it. That is the point. She plants fruit trees that won't bear for ten years. The camera pulls back, and keeps pulling.

And something moves in the grass behind her — which is not a curse. It's the only thing that ever made any of this mean anything at all.

Why it works

  • The theme is an actual argument, not a mood. Is goodness worth anything with no reward and nobody watching? The film puts the strongest possible case for no in the villain's mouth and lets him be right about every fact he cites.
  • The antagonist never raises his voice or tells a lie. Every evil thing in this movie is warm, reasonable and patient. The only force that ever shows up in the open arrives in the last twenty minutes.
  • It plays to two audiences without compromising for either. A faith audience gets scripture kept faithfully, an Antichrist drawn from Revelation rather than invented, and an ending that is unambiguous grace. A secular audience gets a cold survival drama where the miracle is a bookkeeping discrepancy nobody can prove.
  • The budget is where it should be. A hundred minutes of forest, mud and faces; one enormous final act. Contained until it isn't.
