Divinity

Four girls in a car. Windows down. "Highway to Hell."

The one driving stops singing.

Then the one beside her.

The song keeps playing.

Everyone taken leaves a body where they stood. No wound. No cause. Nothing a doctor can find.

The devout drop beside men who never believed anything. One church empties and the one across the road doesn't.

Enough to feel like judgment. Not enough to prove.

Everyone agrees on it anyway.

They were judged. They failed. Nothing is coming for them.

A man at the back of a church watches half the congregation die where they're kneeling.

He came that morning to ask what he'd done wrong. He'd built one company twice and lost it twice, and the second time he'd told people God had a hand in it.

Then somebody notices the priest is still on his feet.

The man says one true thing about that.

It takes forty people a long time. They aren't good at it.

He was right. He never laid a hand on him. A man died anyway.

He doesn't say what he thinks in front of anybody again for four years.

A girl digs three graves in her parents' backyard. Her mother, her father, and her brother.

She was the only one in that house who ever went to church.

A month later, walking north, she finds a field of graves nobody can account for. Squared, mounded, names written on fence pickets.

She knows exactly what she's looking at.

She follows them for a week.

Then she comes over a rise and there's a man standing in a hole.

She picks up the second shovel.

Four years later there's a settlement in the Adirondacks that nobody outside has ever found. A wall, a garden, six children, and four rules an old woman took out of scripture and never pretended she wrote.

Nobody is asked what they did.
Nobody lies about what they do here.
What you break, you answer for.
The gate is never locked.

Not one of them believes there's anybody left to answer to.

They keep them anyway.

The President wasn't taken.

Nobody left him in charge. Everyone else just went.

He gets the power back on. He gets food moving. He's good at it.

Then he looks into a camera.

"If there had been a judgment — would I be standing here?"

A year later a man shoots him twice through the chest on live television, and six hundred million people watch the sheet go over him, and an hour and forty minutes after that he sits up and forgives the shooter by name.

There was no judgment. You are not guilty. Nobody is keeping score.

They love him for it.

In the fourth autumn, men come out of the trees and don't attack.

They set up. Tents, trucks, a field kitchen close enough that the smell reaches the wall.

Then one of them walks up alone and unarmed and is very kind.

Come out. There's food.

He says he'll wait as long as it takes.

Nobody behind that wall has any faith left. Four years of silence saw to that.

So the question he shouts up at them on the last morning is a fair one.

"Who exactly do you think is watching?"

Nobody, she says.

And they stay.

He gives the order on the fifth morning, because the snow has started behind him and he can't go home having failed.

It goes fast.

Then the sky opens.

They come down like a ceiling tearing loose. Gold light out of nothing. Too many wings. Faces that won't hold still long enough to be faces.

And they take the line beside the defenders.

Not in front. Beside.

That morning was a judgment. It was also a selection. Nobody down here could tell the difference.

Some were left because they were judged.

Some were chosen, and never told.

They weren't left behind. They were deployed.

Out on the slope the ones who are only men run, and nobody stops them. What stays isn't men.

Then the dead stand up.

An old woman comes up out of the mud laughing.

They pick their rifles back up, and the only weapon the enemy has left stops working.

Something enormous takes the President apart, and takes the kind man with it, and the last thing he ever learns is that he was never a partner in it.

He was equipment.

Nineteen people held a wall for a law nobody was enforcing.

They were right. Not one of them ever found out why.

Four minutes remain after the field goes quiet.
They belong to two people who never fell.
