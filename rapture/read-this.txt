DIVINITY
A feature screenplay, in development


It happens on a Tuesday morning and it takes about four seconds.

Four girls are driving south with the windows down and "Highway to Hell" up loud. Two of them stop singing. The song keeps playing.

Billions of people die in the same instant, everywhere on earth — and every one of them leaves a body behind. No wound. No blood. No cause of death that any doctor alive can name.

That's the part nobody gets past. If they had simply vanished, everyone would know what had happened. Instead there are a billion arguments and no evidence, and arguments with no evidence get settled the way those always get settled.

And the pattern makes it worse. Not all the children. Not all the believers. Devout people left standing next to atheists who were taken. One church emptied and the one across the road untouched.

Legible enough to feel like a judgment. Inconsistent enough to deny.

Nearly everyone left alive reaches the same conclusion anyway.

They were judged. They failed. Whatever comes next is not coming for them.

John is thirty-seven

, and he's sitting at the back of a church that morning for the first time in his adult life, and not for comfort. He has one company. He's built it to the edge of something twice, and twice it's been taken off him — the second time after he'd already decided, out loud, to anyone who'd listen, that God had helped him get there.

He came to ask what he did wrong.

He gets an answer ninety seconds later, when every faithful person in the room drops dead and he is still standing there breathing.

Then somebody notices the priest isn't dead either. And John says a true thing out loud about that. And forty ordinary people beat the man to death on the floor of his own church.

John is right. John never touches him. A man dies anyway.

He spends the next four years unable to make a decision in front of anybody.

Emma is twenty-one.

Her entire family was taken and she was not, and she was the only person in that house who ever went to church. She digs three graves in her parents' backyard over two days.

Which is why, a month later, walking north through a dead country, she knows exactly what she's looking at when she comes across a field full of graves that nobody can explain.

She follows the work north for a week.

Then she comes over a rise, and there's a man standing in a hole.

She picks up the second shovel. Neither of them says anything.

Four years later there are forty-one people living in six million acres of Adirondack forest, behind a wall they built themselves, under four rules an old woman took straight out of scripture and never once pretended she'd written.

Nobody is asked what they did.

Nobody lies about what they do here.

What you break, you answer for.

The gate is never locked.

Not one of them believes there is anybody left to answer to.

They keep the rules anyway.

And the world outside has a President.

He wasn't taken. He didn't seize anything, either — everyone around him simply went, and left him standing in the same room he'd been standing in on Monday with nobody left on the continent who could tell him no.

People had liked him for twenty years. He turns the grid back on. He gets food moving. He is genuinely good at it and they are not fools for loving him.

Then he stands in front of a camera and asks the only question that matters:

"If there had been a judgment — would I be standing here?"

A year after that, a man shoots him twice through the chest on live television. Six hundred million people watch the sheet go over him.

An hour and forty minutes later, he sits up.

Nobody needs a doctrine after that. He gives them one anyway.

There was no judgment. You are not guilty. There is nobody keeping score.

To a planet drowning in guilt, that is mercy. It is also permission. And to forty-one people in a forest, it is an order to stand down.

In the fourth autumn, four hundred men come out of the trees and do not attack.

They set up. Tents, trucks, a field kitchen close enough that the smell of it reaches the wall.

And a quiet, courteous man walks up alone and unarmed and offers everyone inside exactly what he once offered a frightened girl at a checkpoint. You were never judged. You're not guilty. Nobody is keeping score.

Come out. There's food.

He says he'll wait as long as it takes.

Nobody on that wall has any faith left. Not one of them believes that anyone is coming, and they have four years of silence to prove it.

So the question he shouts up at them on the last morning is a fair one, and every fact in it is true.

"And who exactly do you think is watching?"

She tells him: nobody.

And they stay anyway.

The last twenty minutes are why it's called Divinity.
