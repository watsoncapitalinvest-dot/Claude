DIVINITY
A feature screenplay, in development


Tuesday morning. Four girls in a car, windows down, "Highway to Hell" up loud.

Two of them stop singing.

The song keeps playing.

Billions die in the same second, everywhere on earth. Every one of them leaves a body behind. No wound. No cause. Nothing any doctor can find.

If they'd vanished, the world would know what it was.

Instead there's an argument and no evidence, and that kind of argument gets settled with hands.

The pattern doesn't help. Devout people left standing beside atheists who were taken. One church emptied and the one across the road untouched.

Enough to feel like judgment. Not enough to prove.

Everyone reaches the same conclusion anyway.

They were judged. They failed. Nothing is coming for them.

John is thirty-seven.

He's at the back of a church that morning for the first time in his life, and not for comfort. He's built one company to the edge of something twice and lost it twice — the second time after he'd told people out loud that God had helped him.

He came to ask what he did.

Ninety seconds later every faithful person in the room is dead and he's still breathing.

Then somebody notices the priest isn't dead either.

John says one true thing about that.

Forty ordinary people beat the man to death on the floor of his own church.

John was right. John never touched him. A man died anyway.

He doesn't make a decision in front of anybody for four years.

Emma is twenty-one.

Her whole family was taken and she wasn't, and she was the only one in that house who ever went to church.

She digs three graves in her parents' backyard.

Which is why, a month later, walking north, she knows exactly what she's looking at when she finds a field full of graves nobody can explain.

She follows the work for a week.

Then she comes over a rise, and there's a man standing in a hole.

She picks up the second shovel. Neither of them says a word.

Four years on: forty-one people in six million acres of Adirondack forest, behind a wall they built, under four rules an old woman took out of scripture and never pretended she wrote.

Nobody is asked what they did.

Nobody lies about what they do here.

What you break, you answer for.

The gate is never locked.

Not one of them believes there's anyone left to answer to.

They keep the rules anyway.

The President wasn't taken.

He didn't seize anything. Everyone around him simply went, and left him standing there with nobody on the continent who could tell him no.

He turns the grid back on. He moves food. He's good at it.

Then he looks into a camera and asks the only question that matters.

"If there had been a judgment — would I be standing here?"

A year later a man shoots him twice through the chest on live television. Six hundred million people watch the sheet go over him.

An hour and forty minutes later he sits up.

There was no judgment. You are not guilty. Nobody is keeping score.

To a world drowning in guilt, that's mercy.

It's also permission.

Fourth autumn. Four hundred men come out of the trees and don't attack.

They set up. Tents, trucks, a field kitchen close enough to smell from the wall.

A quiet, courteous man walks up alone and unarmed and offers everyone inside what he once offered a frightened girl at a checkpoint.

Come out. There's food.

He says he'll wait as long as it takes.

Nobody on that wall has any faith left. Four years of silence saw to that.

So his last question is a fair one.

"And who exactly do you think is watching?"

Nobody, she tells him.

And they stay anyway.

THE REST IS THE ENDING.

- - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - -

He gives the order on the fifth morning. The snow has started behind him and he can't go home having failed.

It goes badly. It goes fast.

Then the sky opens.

They come down like a Renaissance ceiling tearing loose. Gold light with no source. Too many wings. Faces that won't resolve.

And they take the line beside the defenders.

Not in front. Beside.

Because that morning was a judgment — and it was a selection, and nobody down here could ever tell which was which.

Some were left because they were judged.

Some were chosen. Four years ago. And never told.

They weren't left behind. They were deployed.

The men who are only men break and run, and nobody stops them. What stays standing isn't men.

And then the dead get up.

An old woman stands out of the mud, fading at the edges, laughing.

They pick their rifles back up, and the enemy's last weapon stops working.

Something enormous takes the President apart, and it takes the quiet man with it — and the last thing he ever learns is that he was never a partner in any of this.

He was equipment.

Nineteen people held a wall for a law nobody was enforcing.

They were right. Not one of them ever found out why.

Four minutes remain after the field goes quiet.
They belong to two people who never fell.
