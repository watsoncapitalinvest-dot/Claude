The Hairy Gumbas is the hottest manager in the Mos Eisley chat. Not the loudest — he is ninth of sixteen for sheer volume — the hottest: the highest share of his conversations carrying a word off the argument list. He does not accept the number. That is a reasonable position to take about a measure this blunt, so this page does not restate the finding. It tries to break it.

Three defences are available to him, and all three are testable. The chart could be catching the other man’s words and billing them to him. He could simply be talking to the angriest people in the league. Or the whole thing could be riding on the two softest words on the list. Each gets its own figure below, and one of them lands.

Test Zero — The Ranking Itself
Test One — Whose Words Are They?

This is the objection worth taking seriously. A volley is heated if either of its two messages carries a word, so a man who never types one can still sit in a hot cell all day — the measure would be recording what is being said to him. So split it: in each of his 410 heated volleys, whose message actually contained the word?

Test Two — Is He Just Talking To Hot People?

The second defence is better made than the first. Heat is contagious in a group chat: if a man spends his time with the four most argumentative managers in the league, his own rate will climb without him doing anything. So work out what his rate should be. Take every pairing he is in, weight it by how often that partner runs hot with everybody else, and add it up. That is the rate a neutral man in his seat would post.

Test Three — Is It All Soft Words?

The third defence is the one that lands, at least partly. The list cannot tell an insult from a quotation of one, and two of its entries — “wrong” and “joke” — do an enormous amount of perfectly ordinary work in a fantasy football chat. If his heat were mostly those two, the number would be an artefact.

Test Four — Was It One Bad Year?
What The Desk Concedes

Two things, without being asked. His two hottest-looking pairings are not on the grid at all: against Still The Creamiest he runs 9.5 per cent and against The Jet-I 8.6, and both are suppressed for resting on too few volleys to mean anything. If anyone has waved those figures at him, they did not come from here.

And heat is a small input to the rivalry score, not a headline. This desk has never published a line of chat on the strength of it and does not intend to. The measure says how often argument words turn up near a man. It does not say he is unpleasant, it does not say he started it, and it cannot read a room.

What He Raised

He makes two claims and they are both checkable, so they were both checked. The first is that the figures are drawn from every WhatsApp conversation he is in, including a political chat he says ran off the rails for three years. If that were true the number would be worthless, because it would be measuring an argument about the news and filing it under fantasy football.

The second claim is the sharper one, and he named names: that the Wookie, the Beavers and the Dragons are the ones throwing heat at him. That is exactly the question Test One answers, so here it is broken out partner by partner — who typed the word, in every pairing he is in.

Where he is right: the words do fly both ways, and nothing here says otherwise — the other man carried a word in 161 of his 410 heated volleys, which is a lot of incoming. He is also right that a group chat can hold a three-year argument that has nothing to do with football. That argument simply is not in this data, so it cannot be the explanation for a number built entirely out of the league chats.
