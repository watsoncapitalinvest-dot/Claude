Portrait, 1024x1536 (2:3), JPG. Photographic, cinematic, editorial.


SHARED STYLE — apply to every weekly cover so the run reads as one magazine

A single dramatic American football image, shot like a magazine cover: one
clear subject, shallow depth of field, stadium floodlights, night or late
afternoon. Warm tungsten and amber light against deep near-black shadow, with
a cold blue-white rim from the floodlights. Rich, expensive, moody. Palette:
near-black, deep red #c20f16, gold #d8b45a, warm amber, cream.

Generic football only. No real team names, no real team colours or uniforms,
no league marks, no sponsor boards, no jersey numbers that read as a specific
player. Plain unmarked kit in dark neutral tones with red or gold trim.

No lettering anywhere in the frame. No words, no numbers, no logos, no
watermarks, no scoreboard text, no banner text. The masthead, the issue line,
the week seal, the cover lines and the league shield are all composited on top
afterwards and must not be drawn.


COMPOSITION — load-bearing, the layout depends on it

Top 20 per cent of the frame: keep it simple and uncluttered. A cream masthead
panel sits across it and a red circular seal overhangs its lower left corner.
Sky, empty stand, or soft floodlight haze is ideal here. Nothing important.

Middle band, from 22 to 58 per cent of the height: this is the subject. Centre
it here, slightly above the vertical middle. It must read instantly at the size
of a phone thumbnail.

Bottom 40 per cent: a dark gradient is laid over it and headline type sits on
top. Keep it visually quiet — turf, shadow, blurred crowd. No faces, no hands,
no detail anyone would miss. It must still look deliberate when darkened.

Verticals matter. The frame is tall and narrow, so compose for a portrait crop
and do not place the subject near the left or right edge.

A running back breaking the goal line at night, shot low and tight from the
side, floodlights flaring behind him. He is mid-stride at full extension, ball
clutched to the chest in one arm, the other arm driving, turf spraying from his
cleats. A defender's outstretched arm enters frame behind him and misses. Dark
neutral kit with red and gold trim, no numbers, no markings. Empty blurred
stands and a blaze of stadium light fill the upper frame as haze. Lower frame is
churned green turf and deep shadow. Shot on a long lens, shallow depth of field,
the runner sharp and everything else falling away. Warm amber key light from the
right, cold rim from the lights behind.


FUTURE WEEKS

Same shared style and composition every week. Only the subject line changes, and
it should picture the moment the issue leads with — a late touchdown catch, a
kicker's follow-through, a quarterback under pressure, a dropped ball on wet
turf. One subject, centred in the middle band, no lettering.

See WEEK-7-COVER-BRIEF.txt. Week 6's painted G.I. Joe register again, but the
cast is the league's own crests rather than real NFL players.

A tug of war across a churned floodlit midfield at night. All sixteen team
mascot characters on one rope, eight a side, every one of them at maximum
strain — and the rope dead level, the marker rag exactly on the centre line,
the mud beneath it scored with heel-ruts running back and forth across that line
many times over. Everything moved all night and nothing ended up anywhere.

All sixteen are anthropomorphic mascots — full body, upright, sports-mascot
proportions, never a crest or a floating head. The descriptions come from the
art on file: docs/TEAM-LOGOS-ART-BRIEF.txt for the five that were written out,
docs/TEAM-LOGO-AUDIT.md for what the other eleven actually show. Where a team's
crest is a photograph rather than a drawn mascot, the brief turns it into a
character — and three of those John specified himself: the Machines are a
terminator, Horsecollars a cartoon horse in a big harness collar smoking a
cigar, Phins Phag a heavyset Italian in Dolphins gear.

This one breaks the "keep the subject away from the left and right edges" rule
on purpose: the rope has to span the frame, so the two nearest characters are
largest at the edges and the lines recede toward the centre.
