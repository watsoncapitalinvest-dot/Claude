Target Share Is The Signal

Fantasy points tell you what already happened. Share of the targets tells you whether it will happen again. The desk said so and then immediately ignored it.

The SCFL NewsRoom · The Player Desk

The rule the desk went in with: when you are deciding whether a waiver pickup is real, fantasy points are the worst available evidence. Two touchdowns in a quiet month will outscore eight honest targets a week, and only one of those repeats. Share of his own team's throws is the signal, because it is a measure of what the offence intends rather than of what bounced in.

Then the desk recommended a receiver on the strength of his fantasy points. Here is the table it should have run first.

The correction, in public

Josh Cameron was recommended here on the back of two touchdowns. His share across the first four weeks was 9, 0, 7 and 8 per cent — flat, small, and exactly the profile the rule exists to catch. Twelve targets in five games is not a role. The touchdowns were the reason to look and not the reason to buy.

Week five is the first thing in his file that argues the other way: six targets out of thirty-nine, 15 per cent, nearly double anything before it. One week is one week. But it is the right kind of week, and it is the number to watch rather than the yards.

The touchdowns were the reason to look. They were not the reason to buy.
What the table says that the points do not
  • Devaughn Vele is the most-used player on the list and the market has him at nothing. A 24 per cent share in week five, rising almost every week, 37 targets through five games — and a dynasty value of zero dollars. He is also 28, which is the catch: that usage is a win-now asset, and a roster rebuilding for next year is buying the wrong year of him.
  • Michael Pittman is being used like a fourth receiver. Twelve targets, two separate weeks at zero per cent, no touchdowns. There was never a version of this where he helped.
  • Mumpfield has one good week and nothing around it. Eight targets in week three is 15 per cent; either side of it is four and six. That is a spike, not a trend, and a bye week means there is nothing new to read.
  • Loveland's share is the real thing on this list. It has climbed from 7 to 12 per cent with a 26 per cent week in the middle of it — which is why the market has him at $43 and rising while his box score says 110 yards and no touchdowns.
The rule, written down so it gets followed

Before recommending anybody off waivers: look up his share of his own team's throws, week by week, and look at the shape of it. A flat small number with touchdowns on top is noise. A rising number with nothing on top is the buy.

Then check the age against the plan. A rising share on a 28-year-old is somebody else's asset.

How this was worked out. Target share is a player's targets divided by his own team's pass attempts that week, counted directly out of nflverse play-by-play for weeks one to five and matched on the NFL's player id rather than on an abbreviated name. Sacks are excluded; a sack is not a target.
