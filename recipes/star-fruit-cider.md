# Star Fruit Cider

A hard cider built on the 18th-century Delaware Valley method, with carambola
(star fruit) standing in for apples.

> **Status of the base recipe.** The Matlack original has not been transcribed
> into this repo yet. The method below reconstructs the standard Quaker
> Philadelphia / West Jersey cider practice of his period — sweat, mill, press,
> ferment, rack, keep in oak, bottle with a lump of sugar. When the family text
> is in hand, put it in `recipes/matlack-cider.md` and reconcile the
> proportions here against it.

A note on the name: in the 18th century "cider" meant apples and "perry" meant
pears. Anything else fermented from fruit was a **made wine** or country wine.
Called strictly, this is a star fruit made wine. It is built and drunk as cider.

---

## Safety — read before making this

Star fruit is not a neutral substitute for apples.

- **Anyone with kidney disease, reduced kidney function, or on dialysis must
  not drink this.** Carambola contains oxalate and caramboxin, a neurotoxin
  that healthy kidneys clear and impaired kidneys do not. Star fruit poisoning
  (hiccups, vomiting, confusion, seizures) is well documented and has been
  fatal. Fermentation does **not** remove either compound — do not assume the
  alcohol or the yeast makes it safe.
- Star fruit inhibits CYP3A4 much as grapefruit does, so it can raise blood
  levels of many common medications. Anyone on statins, calcium-channel
  blockers, immunosuppressants, or similar should treat this the way they treat
  grapefruit.
- Don't serve it to guests without saying what it is. "Cider" sets the wrong
  expectation for a fruit with a real contraindication.

## Fruit

Use **sweet cultivars**. Sour ones carry markedly more oxalic acid and ferment
to something closer to lemon juice than cider.

| Prefer | Avoid |
|---|---|
| Arkin, Kary, Fwang Tung, Sri Kembangan, Maha | Golden Star (unless fully ripe), Newcombe, Star King |

Pick or buy fruit fully golden — green-shouldered fruit is both sourer and
higher in oxalate. Ripen at room temperature 1–2 days until deep yellow with
brown wing edges and clearly fragrant.

**This is the first divergence from the original.** Colonial cider-makers heaped
apples for one to three weeks to "sweat" them — softening the flesh and
concentrating the sugars. Star fruit rots on that schedule. The 1–2 day ripening
above is as far as you can take it.

## Quantities

| | 1 gal (3.8 L) test batch | 5 gal (19 L) full batch |
|---|---|---|
| Ripe star fruit | 9–10 lb (4–4.5 kg) | 45–48 lb (20–22 kg) |
| Expected juice | ~3 L | ~15 L |
| Sugar to add | ~10.5 oz (300 g) | ~3 lb 5 oz (1500 g) |
| Pectic enzyme | ½ tsp | 2½ tsp |
| Campden tablets | 1, crushed | 5, crushed |
| Wine tannin | ⅛ tsp | ½ tsp |
| Yeast nutrient (Fermaid-O or equiv.) | 2 g | 10 g |
| Yeast | 1 sachet | 1 sachet |
| Medium-toast oak (secondary) | ~⅕ oz | 1 oz |

Star fruit is expensive and this is an untested substitution — **make the
1-gallon batch first.** The sugar figure is a starting estimate; the gravity
reading governs.

Yield is ~72% by weight with a press, better if you freeze and thaw the fruit
first (see below).

## Method

### 1. Prepare the fruit
Trim off the brown ridge along each wing — it's bitter and the oxalate runs
highest there — and cut off both ends. Remove seeds; they're bitter. Do not
peel.

### 2. Break the cells
Freeze the trimmed fruit solid, then thaw completely. This ruptures the cell
walls and lifts juice yield substantially. Then chop coarsely, or crush in a
straining bag. **Do not blend to a purée** — that pulls out pectin and you'll
fight haze for months.

### 3. Press
Press in a bag or fruit press. Star fruit is soft and gives up its juice easily.

### 4. Treat the juice
Stir in the pectic enzyme. Wait 30 minutes, then add the crushed Campden.
Cover and rest 24 hours at room temperature.

> The Campden is the modern stand-in for a genuinely period step: burning a
> sulphur match inside the cask to stun the wild flora before filling it.
> Same chemistry, better control. If you want the wild ferment his recipe
> assumes, skip both the Campden and the yeast — but do it on the 1-gallon
> batch, not the 5.

### 5. Adjust
- **Gravity.** Measure. Star fruit juice runs ~1.028–1.032. Add sugar until you
  reach **OG 1.055–1.058**, which lands the finished cider near 7.5–8% ABV —
  in the range of a strong colonial cider. Demerara or turbinado is the closest
  period match; a few ounces of molasses in the 5-gallon batch adds real
  18th-century character, but it's assertive, so hold back if you want the
  fruit forward.
- **Acid.** Target **pH 3.3–3.5**, TA 0.55–0.65% as tartaric. Sweet cultivars
  usually land close. If pH is below 3.1, dilute with water toward your target
  volume rather than reaching for chalk.
- **Tannin.** Stir in the wine tannin. Star fruit has essentially none, and
  without it the cider is flabby — no grip, no finish. Strongly brewed black
  tea works if you have no wine tannin (2 bags for 5 gallons).
- **Nutrient.** Add a third of the nutrient now. Star fruit is low in
  assimilable nitrogen and a stuck ferment is the common failure here.

### 6. Ferment
Pitch the yeast. **Lalvin 71B** is the first choice: it tolerates low pH and
metabolizes some of the malic acid, softening the sharpness. Use **EC-1118** if
you want a bulletproof dry finish, or **Safale S-04** if you want more body left
behind.

Ferment at **60–68 °F (15–20 °C)**. Cooler is better — star fruit's floral,
citrus aroma is delicate and a hot ferment blows it off completely. Add the
remaining nutrient in two doses across the first 72 hours.

Primary runs 7–14 days, down to about 1.010.

### 7. Rack and keep
Rack to a secondary vessel, leaving the lees behind. Add the oak — this is the
one place where a modern shortcut genuinely reproduces the original, since his
cider aged in an oak cask by default.

Age 4–8 weeks, then **rack again off the lees**. Repeated racking is the heart
of the historical method and the single biggest lever on how clean the finished
cider tastes. Rack a third time if it's still dropping sediment.

### 8. Finish
Pick one:

- **Dry and still.** Bottle as-is. Most faithful to the original.
- **Back-sweetened.** Stabilize with potassium sorbate plus a crushed Campden,
  wait two days, then sweeten to taste. Star fruit takes sweetening well — it
  restores the body the low sugar never gave it.
- **Sparkling.** Prime and bottle-condition. The period version is a lump of
  sugar or three raisins dropped in each bottle before corking.
- **Cider royal.** Fortify with 4–6 oz of rum or brandy per gallon. Thoroughly
  documented practice, and rum was the Philadelphia trade good — this is
  probably the closest thing to what a Matlack would have poured for company.

### 9. Age
Give it **3–6 months** minimum. Young, it's harsh and green. It rounds out
considerably.

## What to expect

Green apple and citrus on the nose, floral underneath, with a lighter body than
apple cider even after chaptalization. The acid is the defining feature — expect
something closer to a dry Basque sidra or a sharp white wine than to a
supermarket cider. If it reads thin, that's the fruit, not an error: back-sweeten
or fortify rather than chasing it in the ferment.

## Log

Record for each batch: cultivar, fruit weight, juice volume, OG, pH, TA, yeast,
fermentation temperature, racking dates, FG, and tasting notes at bottling and
at 3 months. The proportions above are a first estimate and want correcting
against real results.
