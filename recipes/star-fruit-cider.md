# Star Fruit Cyder

Matlack's method, run on carambola instead of apples.

Base: `matlack-on-cyder.md` — his two papers of 1807 and 1808. His sequence is
kept (press → ferment → fine → seal), his gauge is used to measure the fruit,
and his three warnings are treated as binding. **Every departure from him is
marked ✎ and explained.** Everything unmarked is his.

---

## Read first — star fruit is not a neutral substitute

- **Anyone with kidney disease, reduced kidney function, or on dialysis must not
  drink this.** Carambola carries oxalate and caramboxin, which healthy kidneys
  clear and impaired kidneys do not. Star fruit poisoning is well documented and
  has been fatal. **Fermentation removes neither compound.**
- Star fruit inhibits CYP3A4 much as grapefruit does. Anyone who avoids
  grapefruit for medication reasons should avoid this.
- Say what it is when you pour it. "Cider" sets the wrong expectation for a
  fruit with a real contraindication.

## Where star fruit sits on his gauge

Matlack weighed a pint of juice against a pint of rain water and recorded the
difference in pennyweights. His range, from the same season:

| | dwt heavier than rain water | modern SG |
|---|---|---|
| Vandever — his lightest named | 11 | ~1.036 |
| House apple | — | — |
| Cooper's sweet russet — richest of 48 years | 24 | ~1.079 |
| **Star fruit ✎** | **9–11** | **1.028–1.035** |

Star fruit lands at or below Vandever, the lightest apple he names. On his own
scale this is a thin must, and everything downstream follows from that.

His work also contains the reason not to stop there. The Virginia crab weighed
*below* the mean of common cider apples and still made cider "not below our best
cyders" — which taught him that "there was some other principle, less open to
detection, on which the excellency of cyder must depend." A light must was not,
to him, disqualifying.

That is a licence to try, not a promise. What made the crab exceptional was that
its must had "much less a tendency to extreme fermentation." Whether star fruit
shares that, nobody has tested. **This recipe is the experiment, not the
result.**

## Fruit

**Sweet cultivars only** ✎ — Arkin, Kary, Fwang Tung, Sri Kembangan, Maha. The
sour ones (Golden Star underripe, Newcombe, Star King) carry markedly more
oxalic acid.

Ripen to deep gold with brown wing edges. Trim the brown ridge off each wing —
bitter, and the oxalate runs highest there — top and tail, and take the seeds
out. Don't peel.

Matlack has no sweating step to skip: he wants the fruit through the mill and
under the press without delay. That happens to suit star fruit, which rots on
the timetable apples tolerate.

| | 1 gal test batch | 5 gal batch |
|---|---|---|
| Ripe star fruit | 9–10 lb | 45–48 lb |
| Expected juice | ~3 L | ~15 L |

**Make the 1-gallon batch first.** Every number below is an estimate against a
fruit he never worked with.

## Mill and press

**Press immediately from the mill.** This is his central and most contested
claim — "the best cyders that I have ever seen, if not all the truly excellent,
has been pressed immediately from the mill": paler cider, more perfectly bright
in the cask, less lees in the bottle. A Society member appended a footnote to
the same page saying this runs against what cider makers generally hold and
urging farmers to test it. **The disagreement is printed on the page and is
still unsettled.**

For star fruit the argument is stronger than it was for apples: the juice
browns quickly and has no tannin to carry a long maceration.

**If the pommace does stand, never measure the wait in hours** — judge by the
heat of the air. The same interval that does nothing in a cool season starts an
acid fermentation in a warm one.

**Lattice the floor**, hair cloth or coarse bagging over it and on three sides
of the crib, "so the must runs off quite fine." A nylon press bag is the modern
hair cloth.

> ✎ **Freeze and thaw the trimmed fruit before pressing.** This ruptures the
> cell walls and lifts yield substantially. It is not his — but it is not the
> warm standing he objects to either. His complaint is against acid fermentation
> starting in the pommace, and nothing ferments at −18 °C. Judge it on his own
> terms: if the juice runs darker than a straight pressing, he was right and you
> should stop doing it.

## Ferment

**Weigh the must first**, his way or with a hydrometer. He wanted farmers using
"Dicas's hydrometer" and said they would hardly credit the difference until they
did; a Society footnote supplies the name — saccharometer.

**Ferment by the weather.** Watch every change, "especially where the change was
from cool to warmer degrees." He refused to give fixed temperatures because
Pennsylvania's climate wouldn't hold them and "our best cellars are too warm, in
the cyder making season."

His one hard number comes from beer, not cider, and it is the most useful figure
in either paper:

> Wort fermented at **65 °F**, drawn off its yeast in time, came spontaneously
> fine and even perfectly bright, soft and free of bitterness. The same wort at
> **76 °F** fixed a cloud no art could remove and grew steadily more bitter
> until it reached "the offensive bitter of the aloes."

Hold 65 °F. Star fruit's aroma is delicate and a warm ferment strips it — his
observation and modern practice agree exactly here.

**Skim the cap when it rises; do not punch it down.** Warmth is fermentation's
first effect; it expands the air in the vesicles of the pulp and floats them up.
"They should then be removed; their return increases the fermentation." He is
explicit about the cost of leaving them: the pulp "will decompose the cyder, and
exhibit to the palate the precise bitter of the apple leaf." Star fruit's ridges
and skin are bitter in the same way.

*This is the one place the recipe contradicts standard modern fruit-wine
practice, which says punch down twice a day. Follow him: skim.*

**To restrain too rapid a fermentation** — rectified spirits of wine, the burnt
taste destroyed by powdered orris root at one ounce to the pint. One spoonful
per hogshead is the safe maximum at any one time, laid on the surface with the
cask full to the bung hole. ✎ Scaled: about **1 mL for 5 gallons**, a quarter of
that for one. It is a surface film, not an addition — do not mistake it for
fortifying, which he forbids below.

> ✎ **What I'd add, and what it costs you.** Star fruit is low in assimilable
> nitrogen and prone to a stuck ferment in a way apples are not. A yeast
> nutrient in three doses across the first 72 hours is insurance he never
> needed. A cultured yeast (**Lalvin 71B**, which tolerates low pH) buys
> reliability at the price of the spontaneous ferment his whole method assumes.
> Both are mine, not his. On a 1-gallon batch, consider going without and
> letting the fruit's own flora work — that is the version he'd recognise.

## Fine

**There must be no fermentation whatever at the time of fining.** So: before the
spring fermentation begins, "which generally happens about the last of March" —
or after it ceases, by which time the cider "most frequently has acquired a
degree of acidity, that renders it not worth fining."

1. **Common staple isinglass**, about five staples to two hogsheads — roughly
   one staple per 25 gallons. ✎ A "staple" is not a weight I can convert
   honestly; use a modern isinglass preparation at the maker's rate and treat
   his ratio as the sanity check.
2. **Pound it and break it into threads.** Dissolve it *in the cider to be
   fined* — beat that cider well, several times a day, for two or three days,
   then strain through a flannel bag.
3. **Pour the fining into the empty cask, then draw the cider off onto it.**
   This "leaves behind, a great part of the sediment, checks insensible
   fermentation, and mixes intimately the cyder with the fining." Modern terms:
   rack onto the fining rather than dosing the full vessel.
4. **Fill quite full, pour the spirits of wine on the surface.**
5. Fine and bright in **six to eight days** — then draw off immediately and bung
   close, or bottle.

**"If it has not been sufficiently fermented, it will break your bottles."**
Take that literally. Star fruit's low nutrient makes an under-attenuated ferment
more likely here than with apples. Confirm a stable gravity across three days
before any bottle sees a cap.

He uses **bright** as a brewer's term, deliberately: not merely fine, but "that
perfect transparency in which liquors are, alone, tasted in their purity."

---

## Seal

Bung close and wax over the bung. Drive the bung home, **bore a gimlet hole
beside the bung hole and leave it open while you cement over the bung** — the
warmth of the cement expands the air below and blisters up through the vent,
"and forever disappoint the attempt to close it." Once the cement has cooled and
hardened, drive a square white oak plug into the gimlet hole.

✎ Crown caps make this moot in bottle. The principle survives the hardware:
after fining, air is the enemy — fill full, seal cold.

## His three warnings

**1. Too much is expected of cider.** "The best Madeira wine will not keep with
less than eight gallons of brandy in the hundred, and twelve is more commonly
used: how then, can it be expected that the juice of the apple, without aid,
should stand through our summer's heat?" A thin must makes a fragile drink. Star
fruit is thinner than anything he measured. Plan to drink it young and cold, not
to cellar it.

**2. Don't fortify.** "Cyder exposes even the smallest mixture of the best
brandy, or any known spirits, more readily than any other liquor."

> ✎ **This overrules my earlier draft.** I had suggested finishing 4–6 oz of rum
> or brandy per gallon as "cider royal." Matlack rules it out in as many words,
> and on a fruit this delicate he is more likely right than I was. Cut it.

**3. Don't boil the must down. Concentrate by frost instead** — "the
concentration of cyder by frost, in our coldest weather, if it has been
previously duly fermented, affords a delicate bottled liquor, that will stand
for years, and improve by time." Boiling "renders yeast absolutely necessary"
and he never met a boiled must "that my stomach did not complain of."

This is his answer to a thin must, and it is a better answer than mine was.

## The two ways to finish a thin must

**His way — concentrate by frost.** Ferment the juice as it comes, thin and
honest, at 65 °F. Fine it. Then freeze the finished cider and draw off the
unfrozen portion, which carries the alcohol and the flavour while the water
stays behind as ice. No sugar ever enters the must. ✎ Freeze concentration is
not distillation, but it concentrates everything — congeners and fusels along
with the alcohol — so concentrate a sound, clean ferment, never a flawed one.

**My way ✎ — sweeten the must.** Raise the juice to OG 1.055 with demerara
before pitching, for roughly 7.5–8% ABV in one pass. Faster, far less fruit,
and the result is a drink he never made. It is not forbidden by anything he
wrote — he objects to *boiling* the must, not to sweetening it — but he never
does it, and the honest label is that this is my recipe wearing his method.

Do his on the 1-gallon batch. It is the one that answers the question.

## What to expect

Green apple and citrus, floral underneath, sharply acidic, and lighter in body
than any apple cider — a thin must makes a thin drink, which is the whole
problem he spent forty-eight years measuring. Pale and bright if you press
immediately and skim the cap; darker and bitter if you don't. That contrast is
the experiment.

## Log

Record: cultivar, fruit weight, juice volume, **must weight in dwt against rain
water** (his measure, so the batches are comparable to his), pH, ferment
temperature, whether you skimmed or punched, fining date and clarity at 6 and 8
days, and tasting notes at bottling and 3 months.

The Society's 1807 footnote asked farmers to test whether pressing immediately
from the mill really makes better cider. Two batches — one pressed straight from
the mill, one held six hours — would answer it for this fruit, 219 years late.
