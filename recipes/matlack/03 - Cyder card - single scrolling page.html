Fining Cyder

Timothy Matlack of Lancaster, 1807–1808 Two papers, both in Memoirs of the Philadelphia Society for Promoting Agriculture, vol. I (Philadelphia: Jane Aitken, 1808). pp. 109–118 — the pommice press, letters to Richard Peters, read 10 March 1807. pp. 268–273 — making and fining, letter to Dr. James Mease, read 8 March 1808.

He almost didn't write any of it down. Fermentation, he says, is less understood than any other subject philosophy or chymistry have attended to; the climate is too variable for reliable directions; and he expected to be blamed when other men's cider failed. His excuse for speaking anyway, at seventy: to reason against fixed prejudices is folly that ought not to be expected beyond the age of 70. He had been trying apples for cider more than forty-eight years by then.

He gives this same 11-to-24 spread in both papers, in pennyweights heavier than rain water. The house apple is next after the russet. Dry seasons give a heavier must, wet seasons lighter.

He wanted farmers using Dicas's hydrometer and said they'd hardly credit the difference until they had one. A Society footnote supplies the name he'd forgotten: saccharometer.

The anomaly

The rule is heavier must, stronger cider. The Virginia crab breaks it — light must, cider equal to the best. Matlack's explanation isn't sugar at all: the crab's must simply has far less tendency to run away in fermentation than common apples of equal weight, so it needs less judgement to restrain. He calls that a decided superiority over every other apple, and leaves the cause for others to guess at.

His press

Built for currant, blackberry, grape and quince wine, sized up for perry, then fitted to cider “far beyond my first intention.”

Any farmer with a saw, an axe and an augur can make the whole thing — a withe will serve for the chain, hickory for the iron pin.

Mill & press

1807

  1. Press immediately from the mill

    His flat contradiction of standard practice. The sooner pressed after grinding, the paler the cider, the more perfectly bright in the cask, the less lees in the bottle. A Society member footnoted this on the page as contrary to general opinion and worth experiments.

  2. Never time the pommace by the clock

    Judge by the heat of the air. The same interval that does nothing in one season starts an acid fermentation in a warmer one.

  3. Line the crib

    Lattice bottom on the perforated floor, hair cloth or coarse bagging over it and on three sides — the must runs off quite fine.

Ferment

Both papers

  1. Keep it cool — his proof is from beer

    One malt wort split two ways. At 65° it came spontaneously fine and perfectly bright, soft, free of bitterness. At 76° it fixed a cloud no art could remove and grew bitter as aloes.65° bright · 76° ruined

  2. Skim the pulp as it rises

    Warmth is fermentation's first sensible effect; it expands the air in the pulp and floats it up. Take it off then. Its return drives the ferment harder, and pulp left in decomposes the cider into the exact bitter of an apple leaf.

  3. Watch the weather, not the calendar

    Attend to every change, above all cool to warmer. He refuses precise directions: the climate is too variable and the best cellars run too warm in cider season.

  4. Restrain a runaway with spirits of wine

    Rectified spirits, burnt taste killed by powdered orris root. Lay it on the surface with the cask full to the bung hole.1 oz orris : 1 pint · max 1 spoonful per hogshead

Fine

1808 · before the last of March

  1. Time it to a dead ferment

    Not the least degree of fermentation may be going on. That means before the spring ferment starts — or after it stops, by which point the cider has usually gone too acid to be worth fining.

  2. Make the fining

    Staple isinglass, pounded and broken into threads. Dissolve in the cider to be fined, beating it well several times a day, then strain through flannel.5 staples : 2 hogsheads · beat 2–3 days

  3. Rack the cider onto the fining

    Fining into the empty cask first, then draw the cider onto it. Leaves much of the sediment behind, checks insensible fermentation, mixes intimately. Fill quite full, spirits of wine on the surface.

  4. Draw off the moment it goes bright

    Fine and bright in six or eight days; draw off immediately, bung close or bottle. Under-fermented cider will break your bottles.6–8 days

Seal

1808 · the gimlet-hole trick

  1. Vent the bung while the cement is warm

    Drive the bung home, then bore a gimlet hole beside it and leave it open while you cement over the bung. Without the vent, the cement's warmth expands the air below, blisters up through the air hole and forever defeats the seal. Once cooled and hardened, drive a square white oak plug into the gimlet hole.

Don't

Expect it to keep like Madeira

The best Madeira won't hold on less than eight gallons of brandy in the hundred, twelve more commonly. Unaided apple juice cannot be expected to stand through our summer's heat.

Don't

Fortify and hope it hides

Brandy blends invisibly into wine over years. Cider does the opposite — it exposes even the smallest quantity of the best spirits more readily than any other liquor.

Don't

Boil the must down

Concentrating by boiling makes added yeast necessary to get it drinkable, and he never met one his stomach didn't complain of afterwards.

Instead

Concentrate by frost

On cider already duly fermented, in the coldest weather: a delicate bottled liquor that stands for years and improves with time.
