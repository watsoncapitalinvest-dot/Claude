THE SOURCES
-----------

Both papers appear in:
  Memoirs of the Philadelphia Society for Promoting Agriculture, vol. I
  Philadelphia: printed by Jane Aitken, 1808

  pp. 109-118  "Account of a new Pummice Press, with some remarks upon
               Cyder making." Two letters to Richard Peters, President of
               the Society, dated Lancaster 7 and 27 February 1807; read
               10 March 1807. Engraved plate of the press facing p. 115,
               keyed description pp. 116-118. Despite the title, this is
               the paper with more cider thinking in it.

  pp. 268-273  "Observations on making and fining Cyder, and on Peach
               Trees." A letter to Dr. James Mease, dated Lancaster
               7 March 1808; read the following day. Continues to p. 279
               on peach trees.

TWO THINGS TO KNOW ABOUT THE TEXT
---------------------------------

Coelho's endnote is slightly tangled. Chapter Three, note 35 gives the
title of the 1808 paper but the page number of the 1807 one. Cite the
two separately. Note 34 dates the CYDER advertisement to the
Pennsylvania Chronicle of 2 October 1769, though the narrative around it
reads as though it belonged to 1764.

The Society did not let Matlack's central claim pass unchallenged. His
insistence that the best ciders are pressed immediately from the mill
drew a footnote from a member on the same page, noting it runs against
what cider makers generally hold and urging farmers to run experiments.
That disagreement is printed on the page and is still the live question.


ON ACCURACY
-----------

Every step and figure in the card and in Part One is Matlack's own.
Nothing has been supplied from other period cider writers to fill gaps.

Two things are NOT his and are labelled as such wherever they appear:
  - The pennyweight-to-specific-gravity conversions in the source note.
    A pennyweight is 1/20 troy ounce, about 1.56 g; against a wine pint
    of water these put 11 dwt near 1.036 and 24 dwt near 1.079. Treat as
    approximate - he never states which pint measure he used.
  - The height of the Virginia crab bar on the gauge. He gives no figure
    for it, only that it sat below the mean of common cider apples, so
    the bar is drawn dashed and marked as indicative.

The peach-tree section of the 1808 paper (pp. 273-279) is summarised
rather than transcribed.
